%% Abstract

\abstract{Deep learning models have demonstrated amazing feasibility in crop disease diagnosis in terms of precision, however, the black-box nature limits the practical usage. Farmers and agricultural professionals need to understand AI diagnosis before they can be used in decision making. In this study, we propose ExplainableCropAI, a system that unites technical performance and real-world applicability. The framework integrates three complementary explainability techniques: Grad-CAM++, a visual heatmap for enhancing localization performance; Monte Carlo Dropout, an uncertainty quantification technique; and LLM-based natural language generation with Phi-3-mini, which produces context-aware, audience-adaptive explanations. The design treats crop diagnosis as a human-centred visual decision-support problem, coupling visual evidence localisation with an uncertainty-aware visual interface and language-based explanation. It was fully explainable and achieved 91.3\% accuracy on a Paddy Doctor dataset of 10,407 photos and 10 disease types, using a ConvNeXt V2-Tiny model, an up-to-date CNN design with depthwise convolutions, LayerNorm and GELU activations. A user study with 12 agricultural stakeholders (extension officers, farmers and students) indicated that the test diagnostic accuracy increased up to 32.4\% and user trust was higher ($p < 0.01$) than prediction-only systems. This highlights the potential to balance the accuracy of categorization with the interpretability of the model in agricultural AI systems.}

\keywords{Visual decision support, Human-centred visual computing, Visual evidence localisation, ConvNeXt V2-Tiny model, Explainable artificial intelligence, Grad-CAM++, Paddy disease detection, Uncertainty quantification, Visual explanations}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

For 3.5 billion people in Asia, Africa and Europe, rice is the most popular food among all staple crops. Rice production is practiced on 167 million hectares globally and 144 million agricultural households rely on rice farming \cite{kamilaris2018deep}. Annual losses from crop failure due to diseases are estimated to be 125-180 million tonnes and this greatly affects the economics of agriculture \cite{savary2019global,rahman2020deep}. More than 70 diseases are known to affect rice crops and many of them show identical symptoms during the early crop appearance stage. Manual diagnosis of disease can be done with mere 23-47\% accuracy depending on the individual’s evaluation and environmental conditions \cite{bock2021severity,nutter1993assessing}. The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) states that pests and illnesses cause an annual loss of 20--40\% of the world's crop production, costing around US\$220 billion \cite{fao2020planthealth,fao2021pests} every year. Visual disease assessment studies have reported intraclass correlation coefficients between 0.60 and 0.85 \cite{mrisho2020accuracy,oerke2020sensor}. Delays from initial observation to sampling to expert consultation along the diagnostic pipeline extend the temporal frame beyond which diseases can be treated effectively, e.g., quickly progressing diseases \cite{ristaino2021persistent}. This is a difficulty that calls for automated and scalable diagnostics.

Deep learning has changed the way it diagnose agricultural illnesses . CNNs have been found to be accurate at > 95\% on datasets of plant disease \cite{mohanty2016using}, but typically on selected datasets in controlled circumstances. Common image processing methods in the early 2000s had an accuracy of 60--75\%, in the 2010s machine learning techniques with handcrafted features could achieve an accuracy of 80--85\% and current deep learning architectures are able to exceed 95\% accuracy on benchmark datasets \cite{liakos2018machine,shafay2025review,akter2024riceleafcnn}. However, when deploying the model to real farm circumstances, a reported degradation of accuracy of 15-22\% is found when trained on selected data sets \cite{mrisho2020accuracy}. ResNet architectures are able to achieve 92--96\% performance on different Crops with the good computational efficiency of the ResNet networks \cite{he2016deep,ferentinos2018deep}.  YOLO-based architectures have also been applied to real-time field detection \cite{fuentes2017robust}. ConvNeXt V2 was launched in \cite{woo2023convnext,wang2024wheatconvnext} in 2023, which merges the efficiency of CNN with the design principles of the transformer, achieving state-of-the-art results by employing depthwise convolutions, LayerNorm, and GELU activation function. 
Rice cultivation has some different type of imaging challenges like the fields are flooded, humidity is high, the canopy is dense with complicated light and occlusion circumstances. Using CNN customization and paddy specific augmentation procedures, Rahman et al. \cite{rahman2020deep} achieved an accuracy of 94.33\%. Transfer learning may be used to reduce the training time and training data, which is done by leveraging the ImageNet data \cite{lu2017identification}.



Although technology is quickly advancing, AI has not been widely adopted in agriculture, partly due to trust difficulties \cite{wolfert2017big,ahmad2024nextgen,gardezi2024aitrust}. Farm decisions have a huge financial impact. A false diagnosis means inappropriate pesticides being used, and the harm to crops leads to losses in income. Acceptance by farmers needs to be conveyed. To solve these problems, there are numerous XAI methods: For the predictions \cite{selvaraju2017grad}, Grad-CAM generates heat maps for the pertinent sections of the image. Grad-CAM++ \cite{chattopadhay2018grad,khan2024potato} developed second-order gradient weighting to improve localization accuracy, and Grad-CAM-based attribution has been deployed on edge devices for real-time crop disease diagnosis \cite{karim2024grapegradcam}. SHAP provides explanations in an intuitive game-theoretic format that supports pixel-level explanations but has high computing costs \cite{lundberg2017unified}; recent work unifies class-activation mapping with Shapley-value attribution for more faithful, theoretically grounded localization \cite{cai2025shapleycam}. LIME, by contrast, provides explanations by perturbing the input \cite{ribeiro2016should} and has low computational costs.

But visual explanations are not enough, confidence estimate is also needed. Uncertainty quantification is performed with several inference passes with active dropout \cite{gal2016dropout}, which may be decomposed into aleatoric (data noise) and epistemic (model knowledge) components \cite{kendall2017uncertainties} via Monte Carlo Dropout. This information of uncertainty is useful in making proper treatment selections. The other is natural language explanations. Template-based solutions produce organized output but lack the flexibility required for complex diagnostic circumstances. They can provide context-aware and audience-adaptive explanations, such as explaining the complete range of disease manifestations, as proven by Large Language Models (LLMs) \cite{abdin2024phi3,zhou2025llmcrop,singh2024llmagri}. Visual-language foundation models have recently been applied to disease diagnosis and human--AI co-decision, underscoring the value of language-based interfaces for expert--user collaboration \cite{yao2025vlmdiagnosis}. Farmers adopt research advances when they trust them, they are culturally acceptable, and they are economically viable \cite{lee2004trust}. Explainability helps to mitigate the trust gap by enabling informed decision making.

In this study, an explainable paddy disease classification system is developed to overcome the trust gap between the applicability of AI and farmers' adoption. The three main challenges are: (i) embedding multi-level explainability mechanisms into state-of-the-art CNN architectures, (ii) evaluating explainability effectiveness for agriculture stakeholders based on user studies, and (iii) optimising the system for edge deployment without compromising accuracy or explainability.

This work approaches crop disease diagnosis as a visual decision-support problem, emphasising visual evidence localisation, uncertainty-aware visual interfaces, and the human-centred interpretation of image-based models. This work makes the following contributions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Visual Evidence Localisation Benchmark}: Under identical settings, we compare six attribution methods (Grad-CAM, Grad-CAM++, LayerCAM, Integrated Gradients, LIME, and SHAP) against expert symptom annotations using IoU, insertion/deletion, pointing-game and runtime, yielding a reusable benchmark for visual-explanation quality in crop-disease imaging.

    \item \textbf{Multi-level Explanation System}: A novel framework that combines Grad-CAM++ for visual heatmaps with improved localization, Monte Carlo Dropout for uncertainty quantification (30 stochastic passes), and Phi-3-mini LLM (3.8B parameters) for context-aware natural language generation, thereby enhancing user diagnostic accuracy by up to 32.4\% in comparison to prediction-only baselines.

    \item \textbf{ConvNeXt V2-Tiny Integration}: First application of ConvNeXt V2-Tiny architecture to agricultural disease detection task, with full support for gradient-based explanation methods (10,407 images, 10 disease classes, and 91.3\% accuracy).

    \item \textbf{User Study Validation}: As a comprehensive evaluation with 12 agricultural stakeholders (extension officers, farmers, students), significant diagnostic improvements were found in user study, statistically significantly higher than black-box systems ($p < 0.01$).

    \item \textbf{Deployment-Optimized Quantization}: For resource-limited farmers, 4-bit quantization for LLM size reduction from 7.6GB to 2.1GB (73\%) with deployment optimization strategies specific to agriculture to maintain explanation quality to enable deployment on smart phones.

    \item \textbf{Audience-Adaptive Generation}: LLM-based explanation system that tailors output complexity to user expertise level, providing actionable recommendations for farmers and technical depth for extension officers.
\end{itemize}

\section{Materials and Methods}\label{sec:methods}

The system comprises five interconnected components (Figure~\ref{fig:system-architecture}): 1. the ConvNeXt V2-Tiny model for disease classification, 2. the Grad-CAM++ module for generating visual explanations, 3. Monte Carlo Dropout for quantifying uncertainty, 4. a natural language generator (LLM) for textual explanations (Phi-3-mini), and 5. a recommendation engine for treatment suggestions. The modular structure allows for optimizing each module separately, while preserving system coherence.

One of the design considerations was deployability flexibility. Multiple operation modes: complete cloud computing, edge computing, hybrid (distributed computation). Also the system provides various levels of explanation based on the competence of the user. Recommendations are straightforward and practical for the farmers and technical assessments are offered in detail for the extension staff. The visual heatmaps are accompanied by uncertainty scores and written explanations, thus constituting a holistic decision support system as depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:explanation-flowchart}.

\subsection{Dataset and Preprocessing}\label{subsec:dataset}

In this work, we use the Paddy Doctor dataset \cite{petchiammal2023paddy} which contains 10,407 high resolution photos ($2448 \times 2448$ pixels) of nine disease categories and healthy plants. Images were captured under real field circumstances with natural light at different development phases to generate a good range of variability for realistic deployment scenarios. The distribution of the classes is shown in Table~\ref{tab:dataset-distribution}. The most frequent class is Healthy (16.9\%) and the least frequent is Bacterial Panicle Blight (3.2\%), a roughly 5:1 imbalance that we control through stratified splitting and inverse-frequency class weighting. This pattern is comparable to the natural illness incidence in rice cultivation. The exact per-class counts in each split are reported in Table~\ref{tab:split-counts}.

All photos were separately assessed by three agricultural experts to validate the data quality with strong inter-rater agreement (Cohen's $\kappa$ = 0.82). This resulted in the reclassification of 89 photographs where experts found the labelling was incorrect and 34 images that were too ambiguous to be confidently categorized. The images were pre-processed by resizing to $640 \times 640$ to balance the cost of computation with the level of detail required for enhancement of subtle disease presentations, Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) to improve local contrast, color space transformations to optimally highlight disease-specific features, and normalization to ImageNet statistics for transfer learning.

\subsection{Deep Learning Architecture and Modifications}\label{subsec:dl-architecture}

We select ConvNeXt V2-Tiny as the backbone model, which is a modern design of CNN that incorporates several significant advances of transformer models while preserving the efficiency of CNN \cite{woo2023convnext}. The ConveXt V2 released in 2023 adopts depthwise separable convolutions that significantly reduce the computational costs, without reducing the representation power. The architecture also switches to using LayerNorm instead of BatchNorm for training stability and uses GELU (Gaussian Error Linear Unit) activations for smoother gradients than ReLU. ConvNeXt V2-Tiny has 28.6 million parameters and offers a compelling trade-off between model size and efficiency, making it ideal for mobile use. Most crucially, ConvNeXt V2 has a rigorously ordered hierarchical feature extraction structure, providing a strong support for gradient-based methods of feature explanation, such Grad-CAM++. The attention mechanism of Vision Transformers produces explanations that are less obvious for agricultural stakeholders compared to saliency maps created by a CNN, despite comparable accuracy when evaluated on benchmark datasets. The depthwise convolution structure of ConvNeXt V2 creates localised feature responses that readily map to interpretable visual explanations, making it very suitable for our explainability aims.

We modified the standard ConvNeXt V2-Tiny architecture in various ways: The last classification head was replaced to predict 10 classifications (9 diseases and healthy). To generate high-quality Grad-CAM++ heat maps, the global average pooling layer was retained before classification to preserve spatial information. To conduct multi-scale analysis, intermediate feature extraction points are added at each stage and statistics for LayerNorm are tuned for Agricultural images, which have distinct statistics from ImageNet dataset used for pre-training. The drop out arrangement is a two purpose setup. separate dropout rates (0.2, 0.3, and 0.5) are used in separate blocks of data during training to minimize overfitting. Active dropout can be utilized to carry out many forward passes to estimate uncertainty from Monte Carlo Dropout without introducing architectural complexity, and also provide uncertainty estimates during the inference phase.

Training was place in three phases. For learning rate 0.001, phase one (epochs 1-20) was used to train only the classification head, freezing the backbone of the network (the part pre-trained on ImageNet). This allowed the network to adapt the classification head task without altering the pre-trained network features. In phase two (epochs 21-60) layers were progressively unfrozen with the discriminative learning rates (1:3:10 ratio), where the earliest layers learnt slower to maintain in contact with the broad characteristics, the later layers more aggressively with the task specific patterns. Phase three (epochs 61--100) was the full fine-tuning (FT) on ag-specific augmentations at lower learning rate (LR). This stage-wise training strategy substantially outperformed end-to-end training from startup.

\subsection{Explainability Mechanism}\label{subsec:explainability}

\textbf{Grad-CAM++ Visual Explanations:} We use Grad-CAM++ \cite{chattopadhay2018grad} which is an improved version of Grad-CAM, which uses second-order gradients to achieve better localization accuracy. In contrast to the standard Grad-CAM, which relies on the first-order gradients to assign weights to contributions from feature maps, Grad-CAM++ calculates pixel-wise weights using both first-order and second-order partial gradients, which allows it to perform better on multiple occurrences of the same object class and provide more accurate localization of disease symptoms in complex agricultural images.

In the Grad-CAM++ process, feature maps are extracted from the last convolutional layer of ConvNeXt V2-Tiny, and second-order gradient information is calculated and used to determine the weights for the weighted combination of feature maps. Such method is able to generate more fine-grained attention maps that give more accurate highlight to disease-affected regions, especially useful when dealing with diseases that have multiple lesions or diffuse symptom patterns. ReLU activation throws away negative contributions and concentrates on features that aid the prediction. It produces a heatmap which is upsampled to a size appropriate to the input image, which is then overlaid for visualization, with the choice of jet or viridis colormaps being determined by user preference testing. Instead, it also tested LayerCAM \cite{jiang2021layercam} to produce explanations from a variety of network layers and fuse these layers for more extensive spatial coverage. LayerCAM proved to be beneficial with early stages of diseases with minor symptoms, meanwhile Grad-CAM++ was more effective with well developed disease symptoms and was chosen as the main explanation method.

\textbf{Monte Carlo Dropout for Uncertainty Quantification:} We use dropout during training and take 30 forward passes with different dropout patterns instead of the typical inference with dropout disabled. Model uncertainty is measured by prediction variance across runs where high agreement means high confidence and high variance means significant uncertainty; this approach has proven effective for uncertainty-aware plant disease diagnosis \cite{pugazhendi2026sugarcanemcdropout}. The uncertainty can thus be decomposed into data (intrinsic uncertainty) and model (extrinsic uncertainty). Epistemic uncertainty is particularly useful, because it tells us that the training data could be valuable. The temperature scaling approach \cite{guo2017calibration} tries to align the empirical accuracy with the raw uncertainty estimations.

\textbf{LLM-Based Natural Language Generation:} Instead of using templates, we use the compact Large Language Model (LLM) Phi-3-mini \cite{abdin2024phi3} to produce context-aware and audience-adaptive explanations. This LLM-based method allows more flexible and natural language generation, which can account for the various symptom clusters and situational nuances of disease that a fixed template can't handle.

Phi-3-mini takes structured input comprising the anticipated disease class, the confidence scores, the uncertainty estimates, the discovered visual features from the Grad-CAM++ analysis, and the target audience specification (farmer vs. extension officer). The model produces explanations depending on the diagnostic scenario and user knowledge level. Farmer-oriented explanations emphasize clarity and actionability (e.g., ``This looks like Leaf Blast with 91\% confidence. Notice the gray oval dots with brown margins on the leaves – this is typical of this fungal illness. 'Consider a fungicide spray in the following 2-3 days. ''). Extension officer explanations add technical depth, including differential diagnostic considerations (e.g. “Primary diagnosis: Leaf Blast (91\% confidence). Brown Spot (5\%) and early Bacterial Leaf Blight (3\%) as differential. Lesion morphologoy shows distinctive diamond shaped progression. Microscopic confirmation is necessary if there is an uncommon edge necrosis pattern. ''). For mobile deployment, we employ 4-bit quantization (GGUF format) which reduces the model size from around 7.6GB to 2.1GB while preserving the quality of explanations. This quantized model provides 1.2-2.0 seconds inference time on contemporary smartphones enabling realistic field deployment without the need for cloud connectivity.

\textbf{Safety and grounding of recommendations.} Because inappropriate pesticide or treatment advice carries economic and environmental risk, the language model \emph{rephrases} rather than \emph{originates} agronomic content. All treatment, prevention, severity and chemical-class statements are supplied to the model as typed fields drawn from a curated knowledge base compiled from public agricultural-extension references (e.g., the IRRI Rice Knowledge Bank and state agricultural-university extension material) and reviewed by the three agricultural experts on our annotation panel; the model is constrained to these fields and does not generate free-form dosages. A template-based generator reproduces the same knowledge-base content verbatim whenever the LLM is unavailable or fails, so the system never relies on unconstrained generation for actionable advice. The system also applies confidence- and agreement-based thresholds: high confidence with high inter-pass agreement yields a direct diagnosis; moderate values add explicit verification-advised wording; and low confidence or high uncertainty triggers a ``consult an agricultural expert'' referral with no firm treatment asserted. ExplainableCropAI is therefore positioned as decision \emph{support} rather than a prescription authority, and any chemical guidance should be confirmed against local agricultural-extension recommendations before field use.

\subsection{Training Methodology and Optimization}\label{subsec:training}

\textbf{Data Augmentation:} A lot of data augmentation helped the model to be robust to actual world fluctuation. Geometric transforms featured random rotations ($\pm15$ degrees), horizontal and vertical flips (50\% chance), random crops at scales 0.8--1.0, and elastic and perspective warping. Photometric augmentations included brightness change ($\pm20\%$), contrast change ($\pm25\%$), hue change ($\pm10$ degrees), saturation change ($\pm15\%$) and Gaussian noise ($\sigma = 0.01$). Domain-specific augmentations proved very useful: generated water droplets on leaves (common in paddy field conditions), synthetic shadows, wind impacts on the blur, and depth-of-field adjustments. Mixup augmentation \cite{zhang2018mixup} was tested but did not increase performance on this classification assignment.

\textbf{Loss Function:} The loss function extends beyond standard classification to incorporate explainability constraints:
\begin{equation}
L_{total} = \lambda_{ce} \times L_{ce} + \lambda_{att} \times L_{attention} + \lambda_{cal} \times L_{calibration} + \lambda_{div} \times L_{diversity}
\end{equation}

The main component is categorical cross-entropy ($\lambda_{ce} = 1.0$) with inverse frequency weighting ($w_i = N/(C \times n_i)$) for class balancing. We added three new terms: the attention consistency loss ($\lambda_{att}=0.1$) to make sure Grad-CAM explanations are consistent for similar inputs, the calibration loss ($\lambda_{cal}=0.05$) to guarantee accurate uncertainty estimation, and the diversity loss ($\lambda_{div}=0.02$) to prevent too much reliance on a few features.

\textbf{Optimization Protocol:} Training was performed with AdamW with weight decay of .01 and cosine annealing learning rate schedule from .001 to .0001. The batch size was set to 16 because of the limit of the GPU memory and the training stability. Gradient clipping (max norm 1.0) stopped gradient explosion. Training was terminated early if the validation loss did not improve for 15 consecutive epochs. Convergence was usually observed between epochs 80--100 and took about 6--8 hours on an RTX 3090. Hyperparameter optimization was performed using Optuna for a Bayesian search over learning rates, loss weights, dropout rates and augmentation intensities.

\subsection{Experimental Design and Validation Strategy}\label{subsec:experimental}

The evaluation process addresses several goals such as classification accuracy, explanation quality, assessment of explanation value and trustworthiness in a user research, and comparison with baseline procedures. The dataset was divided using stratified sampling to keep the class distribution constant. The division was 70\% for training (7,285 images), 15\% for validation (1,561 images) and 15\% for testing (1,561 images). The split is performed at the image level with a fixed random seed; near-duplicate images, identified by perceptual hashing (Hamming distance $\leq 5$), are kept within a single split to prevent leakage, and per-class proportions are preserved in every split. Stratification was used to avoid possible bias in the evaluation sets for certain disease groups. In addition, 5-fold cross validation is performed, stratified sample in each step. This approach generated five independent experiments with confidence ranges for all indicators and the opportunity to test the statistical significance of performance differences. Temporal robustness was further tested by withholding 20\% of the photos from each illness class as a temporal validation set. This configuration is designed to emulate deployment settings in which the model may be presented with different illness manifestations or environmental factors than those it encountered in training.

We evaluated the performance using standard classification measures, i.e. accuracy, precision, recall and F1-score calculated per-class and macro-averaged. We also calculated top-2 and top-3 accuracy, since in practical deployment settings, it is sometimes useful to give multiple diagnostic choices especially when co-occurrence of diseases is conceivable. Evaluating the quality of an explanation involves more advanced techniques than the usual accuracy measurements. To evaluate heatmap alignment, three agricultural experts independently delineated disease-symptom regions as polygon masks on a held-out, expert-annotated subset of the test set; we report inter-annotator agreement (Fleiss' $\kappa$) and consolidate the masks by majority vote. Continuous Grad-CAM++ maps were normalised to $[0,1]$ and binarised at a fixed threshold chosen on a small calibration split to maximise mean IoU, and the \emph{same} threshold and annotation set were applied to every attribution method to keep the comparison fair. The Intersection over Union (IoU) between the binarised heatmap and the expert mask then quantifies localisation quality. To see if highlighted regions have a real impact on predictions, insertion and deletion scores were calculated \cite{petsiuk2018rise}. Point-game accuracy tested how well heatmaps located illness spots identified by experts. The uncertainty quantification study was based on calibration metrics: Expected Calibration Error (ECE), Maximum Calibration Error (MCE) and reliability diagrams. Well-calibrated models produce ratings that are indicative of empirical accuracy rates. Brier scores were also calculated to combine both discrimination (class separability) and calibration (confidence accuracy) into a single measure. 

The quality of the natural language explanation was evaluated by computing the BLEU and ROUGE scores comparing the generated text with expert written reference explanations. Readability was assessed using Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level and Automated Readability Index (ARI) to ensure suitable level of linguistic complexity for the target audience. 
We compared our model against the following baselines: a standard ConvNeXt V2-Tiny without explainability features, ConvNeXt V2-Tiny with Grad-CAM++ only, ConvNeXt V2-Tiny with uncertainty quantification only, alternative architectures (ResNet50, VGG16, Inception-v3, EfficientNet-B0), and traditional machine learning methods (SVM and Random Forest with handcrafted features) to quantify the improvement provided by deep learning. Component contributions were investigated using ablation studies. The comparison of explanation methods showed that Grad-CAM++ was the best performing method but LIME and SHAP were better in some situations. Monte Carlo Dropout was more computationally efficient for uncertainty quantification than deep ensemble approaches \cite{lakshminarayanan2017ensembles}, and ensembles achieved minor accuracy improvements that do not justify the practical deployment limits. The final design decisions were informed by systematic examination of data augmentation, loss function components, and natural language production strategy combinations.


\subsection{User Study Design and Evaluation}\label{subsec:user-study}

The user study assessed system effectiveness with agricultural stakeholders in realistic decision-making scenarios. The participants were recruited from three expertise groups that consisted of four agricultural extension officers with formal plant pathologist training, four experienced rice farmers with more than 10 years of rice cultivation experience, and finally four agricultural science students as beginner users. Recruitment was supported by relationships with local agricultural departments, farmer cooperatives, and the university’s agricultural science programs. The sample size is small but balanced across levels of competence to allow a preliminary evaluation.

The study collected demographic data such as age, education level, years of experience in agriculture, familiarity with technology and prior use of AI-based diagnostic tools. These variables allowed us to analyze how user attributes influence system engagement and trust formation. We used a within-subjects experimental design, where each participant evaluated three system configurations: (1) a baseline system with only predictions, (2) a system augmented with Grad-CAM++ visual explanations, and (3) the full system with visual explanations, uncertainty indicators, and natural language descriptions. The order of presentation was counterbalanced across participants to compensate for learning effects .
Each participant was asked to rate 20 photos of paddy diseases in different disease types and severity levels. Participants independently made a first diagnosis with a confidence rating, and then, if they wished, a revised assessment using the system configuration allocated for each image. They then graded system credibility, explanatory utility and possibility of field adoption using standardized scales. Each session took around 60-90 minutes including the setup and the post-task interview. Individual sessions ensured individual replies and allowed for frank feedback.
Following the task-based evaluation, semi-structured interviews were carried out, which involved questions regarding interface usability, explanation comprehensibility, trust factors, comparison to current diagnostic techniques, perceived strengths and limits, and requested enhancements. These interviews were audio recorded (with participant consent) and transcribed for thematic analysis. This provided qualitative insights to complement the quantitative performance indicators.

\subsection{Computational Environment and Infrastructure}\label{subsec:computation}

Development and training were performed on a deep learning workstation with an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU (24GB VRAM) and CUDA 11.8 support, allowing training of ConvNeXt V2-Tiny with batch sizes up to 32. Data pretreatment and analysis duties were handled by the CPU (Intel Core i9-10900K with 32GB RAM).The software environment consisted of the deep learning tools PyTorch 1.13.0 with torchvision 0.14.0, the classic machine learning baseline scikit-learn 1.1.0, and the image processing tools OpenCV 4.6.0. To keep track of the training process systematically, we used Weights \& Biases (wandb) for experiment tracking and hyperparameter optimization. For deployment testing, we tested on different hardware setups to test realistic deployment scenarios. We tested on an NVIDIA Jetson Xavier NX for edge computing assessment, different cellphones (Android and iOS) for mobile deployment testing, and regular laptops for user study sessions. System behavior was thoroughly confirmed in different computing settings through testing on several platforms.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Classification Performance Analysis}\label{subsec:classification-performance}

The overall accuracy of the classification on the test set is 91.3\%, the precision is 90.7\%, the recall is 90.2\% and the F1-score is 90.4\%. Therefore the following results can be interpreted: Claims of accuracies above 95\% are usually based on a well-controlled laboratory dataset and ideal imaging settings. The Paddy Doctor set has realistic field settings with water droplets, various angles, and inconsistent light with accuracy of 91.3\%, which is a very good result for implementation in real applications. The suggested CNN results in better accuracy when compared with the previous technique based on ResNet50 (85.2\%) proving the success of the proposed CNN in the identification of agricultural disease.

Five fold cross validation with a mean accuracy of 90.8\% with standard deviation of $\pm 1.1\%$ confirmed the stability of the model. The small standard deviation indicates that the model is not learning the characteristics of this data set but rather is generalizing. An examination of top-k accuracy reveals that clinical decision support can be used. The top-1 accuracy was 91.3\%, top-2 accuracy was 96.8\%, and top-3 accuracy was 98.4\%. This means that if the first guess is inaccurate, the correct answer is often among the top three supplied, allowing for the presentation of numerous solutions for the expert to adjudicate. There is instructive variety in analysis for each class. The best classification result was obtained for the healthy class (precision 99.3\%, recall 98.9\%, F1 99.1\%); this is particularly essential since false positives will result in wasteful pesticide consumption, with economic and environmental implications. Leaf Blast obtained the highest disease classification ratings (precision: 92.1\%, recall: 90.8\% and F1: 91.4\%) due to the characteristic circular form of the lesions with gray centers and brown edges. Brown Spot also performed well with the same concentric ring patterns as its visual signatures for all phases of the disease (precision: 89.3\%, recall: 87.6\%, F1: 88.4\%). Bacterial leaf blight was more difficult because to larger morphological variety of elongated lesions with wavy margins (precision: 86.7\%, recall: 84.2\%, F1: 85.4\%).

The largest amount of uncertainty was between bacterial leaf blight and bacterial leaf streak (12.3\%). Both bacterial leaf blight and bacterial leaf streak showed elongated lesions along the margins of the leaves with subtle distinguishing features such as width of lesions and margin features and even experts diagnosticians disagreed on some of them and our annotation validators also noted disagreement in several cases. Since the tungro virus can express a wide spectrum of symptoms depending on plant variety, age, virus strain and environmental factors, its detection had moderate performance (Precision: 81.4\%, Recall: 79.8\%, F1: 80.6\%). Patterns of orange yellow discoloration are not as clear as lesion diseases.

By examining the detailed confusion matrix, there are some patterns of systematic errors that give clues as to the behaviors of the model and how to improve it. The confusion matrix (see Figure~\ref{fig:confusion-matrix}) shows that the overall accuracy for the classification is good; this can be seen from the high number of cells in the diagonal, and a few misclassifications can be seen in the off-diagonal parts. The maximum confusion is seen in morphologically comparable pairings of illnesses such as bacterial leaf blight with bacterial leaf streak (8.7\% confusion bidirectional), brown spot with narrow brown leaf spot (6.4\% confusion) and early stage Hispa with Tungro (5.2\% confusion). certain patterns of confusion are similar to what is seen in plant pathology, where plant disease specialists also have problems telling certain pairings of diseases apart when they do not have supplementary contextual information. In numerous cases the patterns of perplexity are asymmetric, showing systemic bias in the decision making of the model. For instance, Bacterial Leaf Streak was misclassified more times as Bacterial Leaf Blight (11.2\%) than as Bacterial Leaf Streak (6.3\%). This may be due to the fact that the model is able to capture more characteristic features of Bacterial Leaf Blight as it occurred more times in the training dataset.

The severity-based research shows that the classification accuracy is lower for the early diseases (78.4\%) compared to the advanced diseases (91.7\%). The model has been successful in predicting severe disease symptoms, but has not demonstrated strong success in early identification—this is critical for successful therapy, and warrants additional studies.

\subsection{Explainability Analysis}\label{subsec:explainability-analysis}

The quality of the Grad-CAM++ explanation is extremely well correlated to previous knowledge and agriculture relevance. The quantitative assessment measures were calculated using IoU for the illness regions annotated by the experts for all disease categories. The mean IoU obtained was $0.68 \pm 0.12$. This performance result suggests that the visual explanations have successfully identified sections of the image that are relevant to the disease and not unimportant details of other parts of the image. The examination of insertion scores (change in performance with the highlighted region emphasized) yielded a mean score of 0.72, indicating that the regions indicated as important by Grad-CAM++ do contribute to the model’s prediction. Moreover, as the deletion score (0.21) indicates, the explanations are truthful because the removal of highlighted sections has a significant impact on model performance.

The accuracy of the point-game results for the assessment of the location of the highlighted diseases was 81.4\%, and the explanatory heatmaps were generated by agricultural specialists. This factor is a direct check of whether the explanations pertain to true medical symptoms, or to coincidences or to an ordinary trait. Disease-specific angle of study shows that there is variability in the quality of explanation between different presentations of diseases. Lesion based diseases (Leaf Blast, Brown Spot, Bacterial Leaf Blight) have superior explanation scores (mean IoU: 0.74) than systemic diseases with diffuse symptoms (Tungro, Downy Mildew) which have inferior explanation scores (mean IoU: 0.58). This pattern is seen in the natural explanatory advantages of diseases with distinct, readily apparent symptoms located in a specific area. Figure~\ref{fig:cam-examples} shows representative Grad-CAM++ visualisations on real paddy field images, where attention concentrates on the visible lesions in confident cases and the system defers to expert verification when the symptoms are atypical. Monte Carlo Dropout uncertainty quantification gives well-calibrated confidence estimates that correspond flexually and suitably to the precision of prediction. The Expected Calibration Error (ECE) study showed relatively acceptable calibration. The ECE values suggest a reasonable relationship between confidence scores and accuracy rates.

Results from the reliability diagram analysis demonstrates that there is high agreement on the relation between predicted confidence and actual accuracy set for a range of confidence levels. There is good discrimination between the high and low confidence predictions ($<0.5$), with 94.2\% accuracy for high confidence (c>0.9) and 67.8\% accuracy for low confidence (c<0.5), for practical decision maker applications. We find epistemic uncertainty (model uncertainty) is responsible for 60\% of the predictive uncertainty while aleatoric uncertainty (inherent data noise) is responsible for 40\% of predictive uncertainty, using the method of uncertainty decomposition. The decomposition implies that more training data might significantly reduce the uncertainty, offering a way of defining priorities for data collection in the future, as well as for defining the model's scope. 

The uncertainty estimates obtained by performing reverse expert uncertainty analysis are found to be greater for morphologically complex diseases (Tungro, Bacterial diseases) than for diseases with distinctive visual signatures (Leaf Blast, Brown Spot). The pattern suggests that uncertainties do take into account the difficulty of diagnosis and that the user can use these uncertainties to match up uncertainty with expert consultation. To guarantee clarity and ensured agricultural relevance, there are automated measures and expert evaluations for natural language explanation quality assessment. The BLEU score for comparing with expert written reference explanations was 0.73, which shows good semantic similarity with low language distance from the reference texts. 

Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level analysis ensured optimal readability: for farmer-oriented explanations Flesch-Kincaid Grade level was 8.2, while the extension officer explanations' score was 11.4, which matched the reader's scores. These scores allow explanations to be accessible for all users but give the required technical detail to various user groups. Three agricultural experts reviewed 100 generated explanations for accuracy, relevance and clarity and provided expert judgement. Expert evaluation of 100 explanations for accuracy, relevance and clarity by three agricultural experts. The overall scores for accuracy, relevance and clarity were averages of 4.2/5.0, 4.0/5.0 and 4.1/5.0 respectively, reflecting good performance on the evaluation criteria. Specific feedback noted the importance of relating visual explanations to the text explanations and the importance of giving indicators of uncertainty next to diagnostic statements.

\subsection{Comparative Analysis with Baseline Methods}\label{subsec:comparative}

We compared ConvNeXt V2-Tiny against a broad set of baselines under identical splits, augmentation, and training budget (Table~\ref{tab:baselines}): classic CNNs (ResNet50~\cite{he2016deep}, VGG16~\cite{simonyan2015vgg}, Inception-v3~\cite{szegedy2016inception}), an efficient CNN (EfficientNet-B0~\cite{tan2019efficientnet}), recent lightweight vision models (MobileViT-S~\cite{mehta2022mobilevit}, EfficientFormer-L1~\cite{li2022efficientformer}), a fine-grained recognition baseline (API-Net~\cite{zhuang2020apinet}), and traditional machine-learning methods with handcrafted features. ConvNeXt V2-Tiny achieved the best accuracy (91.3\%) at a competitive parameter count (28.6M) and inference time (28ms). ResNet50 (85.2\%) and Inception-v3 (86.1\%) were markedly lower, and Inception-v3 incurred a longer inference time (47ms) that limits mobile viability. EfficientNet-B0 (83.7\%) and the lightweight transformers (MobileViT-S 84.6\%, EfficientFormer-L1 86.4\%) traded accuracy for size and latency. The fine-grained baseline improved over plain CNNs (87.2\%) but did not match the modern backbone, while incurring higher latency. Crucially, the depthwise-convolution structure of ConvNeXt V2 yields cleaner Grad-CAM++ localisation than EfficientNet-B0 and Inception-v3, whose architectures degrade attribution quality. Traditional machine-learning baselines with handcrafted features were far weaker (SVM-HOG 71.2\%, Random Forest 68.7\%, k-NN-SIFT 64.3\%), confirming that deep representations are essential for this task.

We further isolated the contribution of each design choice through an ablation study (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). Starting from the plain backbone (89.4\%), the multi-scale feature pyramid~\cite{lin2017fpn} (+0.7\%), attention-consistency loss (+0.5\%), diversity loss (+0.2\%), calibration loss (+0.2\%, which also lowered ECE from 0.142 to 0.087), and class-balanced weighting (+0.3\%) each contributed measurable benefit, reaching the full 91.3\%. The uncertainty, language-explanation, and recommendation components do not change classification accuracy; their benefit is measured in the user study (Section~\ref{subsec:user-study-results}), where visual explanations added up to +6.1\% and the full multi-channel system up to +32.4\% to human diagnostic accuracy over a prediction-only baseline. As indicated previously, the two methods of explanation are carefully contrasted in Table \ref{tab:visual-explanation} and hazards of trading explanation ways to boost which type of model interpretability are highlighted. LIME achieved an IoU of 0.61 on expert annotations. However, it generated different explanations for comparable inputs, making its conclusions less dependable for some applications such as agriculture where consistent results are required for user approval. On the other hand, SHAP explanations had a higher computational overhead, taking 3.2s for an explanation, compared to 0.4s for Grad-CAM, with a similar IoU (0.64) to Grad-CAM. The per-pixel attributions that SHAP produces are a detailed explanation, but this can be too much information for someone in an agricultural context who wants a simplified explanation to take some action.
Vanilla CAM~\cite{zhou2016cam} was lower because to architecture limitations and obtained inferior quality of explanation (IoU: 0.57). However, CAM provides coarser explanations that are not sufficient to show the exact location of the disease in complicated agricultural imagery. Integrated gradients explanations provide high-quality attributions (IoU: 0.71) but are very computationally expensive (2.8s per explanation), therefore preventing real-time use. It has been described as technically superior in some areas, but computational constraints make it infeasible for resource-poor agricultural environments.

\subsection{User Study Results and Human-AI Interaction Analysis}\label{subsec:user-study-results}

The user study was conducted with 12 users who were divided into three groups: 4 agricultural extension officers with an average of 9.2 years in agriculture and 4 experienced farmers who had an average of 17.5 years working with rice and another 4 were final year agricultural science students with no prior experience in agriculture.Baseline diagnostics revealed significantly different overall accuracy levels for the various user groups, without the support of AI. The extension officers, farmers and students correctly identified 73.1\%, 67.5\% and 52.3\% of the 20 images respectively. For different levels of expertise in AI diagnosis, the effect is measured against the baseline measures.

During the confidence calibration investigation, the percentage of user confidence was always higher than the percentage of accuracy, thus the overconfidence bias was manifested by the user categories. Farmers had the second biggest mean gap (13.8\%) while extension officers had the lowest (9.2\%). This pattern suggests that uncertainty indications are especially valuable for less trained users who typically are overconfident. The analysis of the system design at the system level revealed that different system designs affected user categories differently. The prediction-only system yielded higher accuracy for all user categories, which included extension staff (+11.3\%), farmers (+14.7\%) and students (+21.5\%). And as these improvements show, the explainability skills are just one aspect of the enormous influence AI can have. The results were further enhanced with the addition of visual explanations (Grad-CAM++ heatmaps), with an increase in accuracy of 3.6\% for extension officers, 5.2\% for farmers and 6.1\% for students (accuracy values of 88.0\%, 87.4\% and 79.9\%, respectively). Qualitative feedback indicated that the visual explanations helped users discover disease signs they were not aware of and increased confidence in AI suggestions when the highlighted areas matched their visual assessment.
The full approach (including uncertainty indications and explanations in natural language) showed the best performance with extension staff 90.5\% (+2.5\%), farmers 90.8\% (+3.4\%) and students 84.7\% (+4.8\%). These were smaller incremental improvements but qualitative feedback indicated the value of sharing ambiguity where uncertainty existed in order to facilitate decision making. The results of the user studies are summarized in Figure~\ref{fig:user-study-results} that shows different explanation modalities affect the user performance across expertise levels and Figure~\ref{fig:explanation-effectiveness} that shows multi-level explanations are complementary for different agricultural decision making scenarios. The integrated user interface (Figure~\ref{fig:user-interface}) shows how uncertainty indicators, natural language descriptions and visual explanations are used to support farmer decision making.
The tool utilized for trust assessment was likert scale of 1 to 5 across many elements such as system reliability, clarity of explanation, confidence in recommendation and likelihood of adoption. Total trust scores varied from 4.1/5.0 for all participants, with extension officers scoring the highest (4.3/5.0), students the second highest (4.1/5.0) and farmers the lowest (3.8/5.0). Explainability features are also found to have substantial effect on the user acceptability, as indicated in the greater trust scores acquired by the complete explanation system compared to the prediction-only systems ($p < 0.01$, Wilcoxon signed-rank test). Trust showed the largest increase in aspects of explanation clarity and recommendation certainty.
The accuracy score of the system was found to be statistically correlated with the trust score (Spearman's $\rho = 0.72, p<0.01$), while the uncertainty level of the system was statistically correlated with the trust score (Spearman's $\rho = -0.58, p<0.05$). The diagnostic-accuracy gains over the prediction-only baseline corresponded to large effect sizes for every stakeholder group (Cohen's $d > 1.2$), and all reported paired comparisons remained statistically significant after Holm--Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons; given the small sample ($n=12$), these findings are reported as preliminary. This is in line with the users' behavior to adjust their trust as appropriate to the system's performance feedback, and reveals the importance of uncertainty communication in the context of calibrated HAI. The semi-structured interviews were analysed thematically and different topics came up, including the usability of the system, effectiveness of the explanation and deployment of the system. The most highlighted positive features were: visual explanation clarity (11/12) and diagnostic accuracy improvements (10/12), and uncertainty messaging (8/12).
Problems and limitations participants identified were occasional description errors (5/12), the need for more context (7/12), and questions about system functioning with different field situations (6/12). These results are used for future development and deployment planning for systems. Qualitative responses indicated that there were differences between user categories. Extension officers emphasised the importance of uncertainty indicators in risk assessment and consistency of explanations in order to be credible to the profession. The farmers highlighted the need for the visual explanation to be clear and expressed interest in the integration with treatment suggestion systems. The instructional explanations were valued by the pupils to acquire skills in identifying sickness.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

This strict evaluation of explainable agricultural AI is amazing 91.3\% on correctness. This is extremely close to the $>95\%$ we have got with black-box models on properly controlled data sets and with a full explainability, indicating that current designs can minimize dramatically the classical performance-explainability trade-off. The employed ConvNeXt V2-Tiny with depthwise convolutions and new normalisation techniques resulted in an improvement of roughly 6\% in the prediction accuracy compared to the previous ResNet50-based methods. Significantly, our analysis showed that the system reached a baseline accuracy substantially higher than that of the extension officials (73.1\%) and the farmers (67.5\%). Cross validation stability (CVS): $90.8\% \pm 1.1\%$ indicates that the model is learning characteristics of the dataset that generalise and not only memorising artifacts.
The results of visual explanation (IoU: $0.68 \pm 0.12$) with the expert annotations validate that Grad-CAM++ is effective in localizing the agriculturally relevant disease areas with superior visual explanations than Grad-CAM. Grad-CAM++ produces more precise attention maps, which are notably beneficial for the detection of numerous lesions in illnesses. Analysis by disease species revealed that lesions with evident morphological symptoms (Leaf Blast, Brown Spot) had greater IoU (0.74) compared to systemic diseases with non-distinct symptoms (0.58). The pattern suggests that the quality of the explanation should take account of some of the qualities of an illness. Explanation faithfulness measures (insertion: 0.72, deletion: 0.21) do demonstrate that visual explanations are actually loyal to the model reasoning process and not just “after-the-fact” explanations. The estimation of the uncertainty was well-calibrated (ECE: 0.087) and offered relevant decision support by means of confidence levels. 
The user study revealed considerable discrepancies between the stakeholders. The largest growth was observed in students in agriculture (+32.4\% with the full system), which emphasizes the explanatory AI systems for educational purposes other than diagnostics. The accuracy gain for extension officers was more modest (+17.4\%) but the use of uncertainty indicators for risk assessment and documenting choices was of major importance underlining the value of uncertainty indicators for extension officers’ work. Farmers reported significant gains (+23.3\%) and expressed a strong desire to have visual explanations that may offer realistic treatment recommendations. Trust scores showed a positive association with accuracy ($\rho = 0.72$, Spearman) and a negative correlation with uncertainty ($\rho = -0.58$, Spearman) , indicating that the user adequately trusted the system according to the indications of system performance.
In terms of deployment, the computational demand is modest, and we report measured and estimated figures separately. \emph{Measured} (on the development workstation, NVIDIA RTX 3090, batch size 1): the classification model runs in 28\,ms at 28.6M parameters and $\approx$115\,MB storage; the attribution and uncertainty overheads are 0.5\,s for Grad-CAM++ and 0.8\,s for 30-pass Monte Carlo Dropout; and the 4-bit (NF4, GGUF) quantized Phi-3-mini, reduced from $\approx$7.6\,GB to 2.1\,GB, generates explanations in 1.2--2.0\,s on contemporary smartphones, enabling fully offline operation. \emph{Estimated} (not directly measured, device-dependent): in-field battery use is approximately 6--10\% per 10--20 diagnoses. On-device classifier latency on a phone or a Jetson Xavier NX edge module will differ from the workstation figure and should be benchmarked per target device. A practical trade-off remains: the offline LLM layer costs $\approx$2.1\,GB of storage and 1--2\,s of latency, which can exceed the budget of low-end devices; we therefore recommend a hybrid deployment in which the classifier, visual heatmap and uncertainty estimate (the safety-critical channels) always run on-device, while the natural-language layer is offline-optional or cloud-assisted. The modular architecture also allows easy integration with existing agricultural information systems, IoT sensors and precision-agriculture platforms.

\textbf{Limitations:} Some limitations need to be considered. Firstly, the assessment is based on the Paddy Doctor dataset of specific geographical regions and may not be generalizable to other rice growing areas with various climatic circumstances, cultivars or disease symptoms. Secondly, the user study was small (n=12) and in a specific context, so these results are preliminary and risk being unrepresentative, which is why the researchers recommend further studies with more users, in different settings, to confirm the results. Thirdly, in contrast to template based approaches, Phi-3-mini does not guarantee correctness in all cases of NLPG, and sometimes wrong words or statements are generated, hence prompt engineering and output checks must be employed carefully. Fourth, Monte Carlo Dropout can provide computationally cheap estimates of uncertainty, but it may miss some uncertainty types, such as uncertainty due to out-of-distribution inputs. Fifth, the 4-bit quantized LLM will require an additional 2.1GB of storage, which may be too much for some low-end devices. Finally, the focus on single-image diagnosis may not be adequate for scenarios where the farmers upload a sequence of photographs of the same plant or field to obtain a more thorough diagnosis.

\textbf{Future Directions:} There are several promising directions that should be pursued. There are methods for explaining more advanced, especially counterfactual and contrastive techniques which are specifically developed for agricultural contexts that could offer more intuitive explanations than current attention-based explanations. Combining multi-modal sensing (hyperspectral imaging and thermal cameras) may provide a huge improvement in diagnostics, particularly for diseases with subtle visual signs. Ongoing learning capabilities would enable the system to adjust to new variants of the disease and new environmental conditions without having to retrain the system completely. Federated learning models might help to improve the model collectively across different farms and regions, while maintaining data privacy, which could be essential for its implementation. Extension of other crop types is a logical next step, as is linking with precision agriculture technologies for automated early warning systems. Multilingual support will be crucial to disseminating to farmers across different linguistic areas and linkages to agricultural insurance schemes may offer extra motivation for uptake and offer useful data for risk assessment.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

In this research work presented a comprehensive framework ExplainableCropAI for addressing the gap between performance and explainability. In this work integrated various explainable AI techniques like Grad-CAM++ for visual explanations with improved localization, Monte Carlo Dropout for uncertainty quantification, and LLM-based natural language generation using Phi-3-mini for context-aware explanations. The system achieves 91.3\% classification accuracy with full explainability which is more then human expert baselines observed in our user study (extension officers: 73.1\%, farmers: 67.5\%). By using ConvNeXt V2-Tiny CNN architecture with depthwise convolutions and GELU activations, provided 6\% more accuracy improvement in comparision of ResNet50-based approaches. Cross-validation stability ($90.8\% \pm 1.1\%$) indicates reliable performance that generalizes across diverse conditions. Visual explanations using Grad-CAM++ align well with expert knowledge (IoU: 0.68), uncertainty quantification produces well-calibrated confidence estimates (ECE: 0.087), and LLM-based natural language explanations demonstrate both clarity and relevance (BLEU: 0.73). The user study demonstrated shows improvements among all expertise levels: agricultural students improved by 32.4\%, farmers by 23.3\%, and extension officers by 17.4\%. Explainability features shows to user acceptance, with the complete explanation system generating significantly higher trust scores compared to prediction-only alternatives ($p < 0.01$). Computational requirements remain practical for mobile deployment (28ms inference, 28.6M parameters for ConvNeXt V2-Tiny, approximately 115MB for the classification model plus 2.1GB for the quantized Phi-3-mini LLM).

Main key contributions of this work are as follows. First, it demonstrate empirically that comprehensive explainability can be integrated with agricultural AI without prohibitive performance penalties. Second, it provided evidence that multi-modal explanations genuinely improve both user performance and trust across diverse stakeholder categories, not only among highly technical users. Third, it present a working technical framework that balances accuracy, explainability, and computational efficiency in deployable form. Fourth, our evaluation methodology encompasses both technical performance metrics and crucial human factors often overlooked in computer vision research.
