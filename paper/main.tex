%% Abstract
%%==================================%%

\abstract{Deep learning models achieve impressive accuracy in crop disease detection, but their black-box nature limits practical adoption. Farmers and agricultural experts require understanding of AI-generated diagnoses before integrating such systems into decision-making workflows. This paper presents ExplainableCropAI, a framework bridging technical performance and real-world usability. The framework integrates three complementary explainability techniques: Grad-CAM++ for visual heatmaps with improved localization, Monte Carlo Dropout for uncertainty quantification, and LLM-based natural language generation using Phi-3-mini for context-aware, audience-adaptive explanations. Using a ConvNeXt V2-Tiny architecture---a modern CNN design incorporating depthwise convolutions, LayerNorm, and GELU activations---the system achieved 91.3\% accuracy on the Paddy Doctor dataset (10,407 images, 10 disease classes) while maintaining full explainability. A user study with 12 agricultural stakeholders---extension officers, farmers, and students---demonstrated significant improvements in diagnostic accuracy (up to 32.4\%) and user trust ($p < 0.01$) compared to prediction-only systems. These results demonstrate that classification accuracy and model interpretability can be effectively balanced in agricultural AI applications.}

\keywords{explainable artificial intelligence, agricultural decision support, deep learning, paddy disease detection, uncertainty quantification, computer vision, visual explanations, precision agriculture}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Rice, one of the most widely consumed staple crops globally, supports 3.5 billion people across Asia, Africa, and Europe. Paddy fields span 167 million hectares worldwide, with 144 million agricultural households depending on rice production \cite{kamilaris2018deep}. Disease-related crop failures cause annual losses of 125--180 million tons, severely impacting agricultural economies \cite{savary2019global,rahman2020deep}. Over 70 different diseases affect rice crops, many exhibiting similar visual symptoms during early stages. Traditional manual disease identification achieves only 23--47\% accuracy depending on assessor experience and environmental conditions \cite{bock2021severity,nutter1993assessing}.

The Food and Agriculture Organization estimates 20--40\% of global crop production is lost annually to pests and diseases, costing approximately US\$220 billion \cite{fao2020planthealth,fao2021pests}. Visual disease assessment studies report inter-rater reliability coefficients ranging from 0.60 to 0.85 \cite{mrisho2020accuracy,oerke2020sensor}. The diagnostic pipeline---from initial observation through sample collection to expert consultation---creates delays exceeding effective treatment windows for rapidly progressing diseases \cite{ristaino2021persistent}. These challenges demand automated, scalable diagnostic solutions.

\subsection{Deep Learning in Agricultural Disease Detection}\label{subsec:dl-ag}

Deep learning has transformed agricultural disease diagnostics. CNNs achieve accuracy rates exceeding 95\% on plant disease datasets \cite{mohanty2016using}, though results typically derive from curated datasets under controlled conditions. Traditional image processing methods achieved 60--75\% accuracy in the early 2000s; machine learning with handcrafted features reached 80--85\% through the 2010s; current deep learning architectures exceed 95\% on benchmark datasets \cite{liakos2018machine,singh2023comparative,garcia2024plantdiseasereview}. However, a 15--22\% accuracy degradation is observed when models trained on curated datasets are deployed in real farm conditions \cite{mrisho2020accuracy}.

ResNet architectures achieve 92--96\% accuracy across different crops with favorable computational efficiency \cite{he2016deep,ferentinos2018deep}. YOLO architectures demonstrate promise for real-time field detection at 140+ FPS \cite{fuentes2017robust,liu2020yolo}. ConvNeXt V2, released in 2023, combines CNN efficiency with transformer-inspired design principles, achieving state-of-the-art performance through depthwise convolutions, LayerNorm, and GELU activations \cite{woo2023convnext,wang2024wheatconvnext}.

Rice cultivation presents unique imaging challenges: flooded fields, high humidity, and dense canopy structures create complex lighting and occlusion conditions. Rahman et al. \cite{rahman2020deep} achieved 94.33\% accuracy through CNN customization with paddy-specific augmentation strategies. Transfer learning from ImageNet reduces training time and data requirements \cite{lu2017identification}.

\subsection{Explainable AI and Trust in Agricultural Systems}\label{subsec:xai-trust}

Despite technical advances, AI adoption in agriculture remains slow due to trust limitations \cite{wolfert2017big,ahmad2024nextgen}. Agricultural decisions carry significant financial consequences; incorrect diagnoses lead to inappropriate pesticide application, crop damage, and economic losses. Explainability is essential for farmer acceptance.

Several XAI methods address these challenges. Grad-CAM generates heatmaps indicating which image regions influenced predictions \cite{selvaraju2017grad}. Grad-CAM++ improves localization accuracy through second-order gradient weighting \cite{chattopadhay2018grad,khan2024potato}. LIME explains predictions through input perturbations \cite{ribeiro2016should}, while SHAP provides game-theoretic pixel-level explanations but incurs substantial computational overhead \cite{lundberg2017unified}.

Visual explanations address only part of the interpretability challenge; confidence estimation is equally critical. Monte Carlo Dropout provides uncertainty quantification through multiple inference passes with active dropout \cite{gal2016dropout}, enabling decomposition into aleatoric (data noise) and epistemic (model knowledge) components \cite{kendall2017uncertainties}. This uncertainty information is essential for appropriate treatment decisions.

Natural language explanations represent a complementary approach. Template-based systems provide structured outputs but lack flexibility for complex diagnostic scenarios. Large Language Models (LLMs) enable context-aware, audience-adaptive explanations that can address the full spectrum of disease manifestations \cite{abdin2024phi3,zhou2025llmcrop,singh2024llmagri}. Research demonstrates that farmer acceptance depends on trust, cultural alignment, and economic viability \cite{lee2004trust,wright2022adoption}. Explainability bridges the trust gap by enabling informed decision-making \cite{german2021co}.

\subsection{Research Objectives and Contributions}\label{subsec:objectives}

This study develops an explainable paddy disease classification system that addresses the trust gap between AI capabilities and farmer adoption. The primary focus is threefold: (i) integrating multi-level explainability mechanisms into modern CNN architectures, (ii) validating explanation effectiveness through user studies with agricultural stakeholders, and (iii) optimizing the system for edge deployment without sacrificing accuracy or interpretability.

This work makes the following contributions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Multi-level Explanation System}: A novel framework integrating Grad-CAM++ for visual heatmaps with improved localization, Monte Carlo Dropout for uncertainty quantification (30 stochastic passes), and Phi-3-mini LLM (3.8B parameters) for context-aware natural language generation that improves user diagnostic accuracy by up to 32.4\% over prediction-only baselines.

    \item \textbf{ConvNeXt V2-Tiny Integration}: First application of ConvNeXt V2-Tiny architecture to agricultural disease detection, achieving 91.3\% accuracy on Paddy Doctor dataset (10,407 images, 10 disease classes) while maintaining full compatibility with gradient-based explanation methods.

    \item \textbf{User Study Validation}: Comprehensive evaluation with 12 agricultural stakeholders (extension officers, farmers, students) demonstrating statistically significant improvements in diagnostic accuracy ($p < 0.01$) and user trust compared to black-box systems.

    \item \textbf{Deployment-Optimized Quantization}: Agricultural-specific optimization strategies reducing LLM size from 7.6GB to 2.1GB (73\% compression) via 4-bit quantization while preserving explanation quality, enabling smartphone deployment for resource-constrained farmers.

    \item \textbf{Audience-Adaptive Generation}: LLM-based explanation system that tailors output complexity to user expertise level, providing actionable recommendations for farmers and technical depth for extension officers.
\end{itemize}

\section{Materials and Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{System Architecture}\label{subsec:architecture}

The system comprises five interconnected components (Figure~\ref{fig:system-architecture}): a ConvNeXt V2-Tiny model for disease classification, a Grad-CAM++ module for visual explanations, Monte Carlo Dropout for uncertainty quantification, an LLM-based natural language generator (Phi-3-mini) for textual explanations, and a recommendation engine for treatment suggestions. The modular architecture enables independent optimization of each component while maintaining system coherence.

Deployment flexibility was a key design consideration. The architecture supports multiple operational modes: full cloud processing for users with reliable connectivity, edge computing for resource-constrained environments, and a hybrid approach that balances computation distribution. Additionally, the system provides tiered explanation depth based on user expertise. Farmers receive straightforward, actionable recommendations, while extension officers can access detailed technical analyses. Figure~\ref{fig:explanation-flowchart} illustrates the integration of visual heatmaps, uncertainty scores, and textual explanations to provide comprehensive decision support.

\subsection{Dataset and Preprocessing}\label{subsec:dataset}

This study utilized the Paddy Doctor dataset \cite{das2020paddy}, comprising 10,407 high-resolution images ($2448 \times 2448$ pixels) of nine disease categories plus healthy plants. Images were captured in real field conditions under natural lighting across various growth stages, providing substantial variability representative of practical deployment scenarios. Table~\ref{tab:dataset-distribution} presents the class distribution: Leaf Blast represents the most prevalent category (16.7\%), while Downy Mildew is the least common (4.9\%). This distribution approximates natural disease prevalence patterns observed in rice cultivation.

Data quality validation involved three agricultural experts who independently reviewed all images, achieving substantial inter-rater agreement (Cohen's $\kappa$ = 0.82). This process resulted in reclassification of 89 images where experts identified labeling errors and exclusion of 34 images deemed too ambiguous for confident annotation. Preprocessing consisted of resizing to $640 \times 640$ pixels to balance detail preservation with computational efficiency, application of Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) to enhance local contrast for subtle disease manifestations, color space transformations optimized for disease-specific features, and normalization to ImageNet statistics for transfer learning compatibility.

\subsection{Deep Learning Architecture and Modifications}\label{subsec:dl-architecture}

ConvNeXt V2-Tiny was selected as the base architecture, representing a modern CNN design that incorporates key advances from transformer architectures while maintaining CNN efficiency \cite{woo2023convnext}. Released in 2023, ConvNeXt V2 employs depthwise separable convolutions that significantly reduce computational costs while preserving representational capacity. The architecture utilizes LayerNorm instead of BatchNorm for improved training stability, and GELU (Gaussian Error Linear Unit) activations that provide smoother gradients compared to ReLU. With 28.6 million parameters, ConvNeXt V2-Tiny offers a favorable balance between model capacity and computational efficiency suitable for mobile deployment.

Critically, ConvNeXt V2's hierarchical feature extraction with clearly defined stages maintains excellent compatibility with gradient-based explanation methods such as Grad-CAM++. While Vision Transformers achieve comparable accuracy on benchmark datasets, their attention mechanisms produce explanations that are less intuitive for agricultural stakeholders compared to CNN-based saliency maps. The depthwise convolution structure in ConvNeXt V2 provides localized feature responses that translate directly to interpretable visual explanations, making it particularly suitable for our explainability objectives.

Several modifications were applied to the standard ConvNeXt V2-Tiny architecture. The final classification head was replaced to predict 10 classes (9 diseases plus healthy). The global average pooling layer was retained before classification to preserve spatial information necessary for high-quality Grad-CAM++ heatmaps. Intermediate feature extraction points were added at each stage for multi-scale analysis, and the LayerNorm statistics were fine-tuned for agricultural imagery, which exhibits different statistical properties than the ImageNet dataset used for pre-training.

The dropout configuration serves dual purposes. During training, graduated dropout rates (0.2, 0.3, and 0.5 after successive blocks) prevent overfitting. During inference, maintaining active dropout enables multiple forward passes for Monte Carlo Dropout uncertainty estimation, providing uncertainty quantification without additional architectural complexity.

Training proceeded in three phases. Phase one (epochs 1--20) trained only the classification head while freezing the ImageNet-pretrained backbone at learning rate 0.001, allowing task-specific adaptation without disrupting pre-learned features. Phase two (epochs 21--60) progressively unfroze layers using discriminative learning rates (1:3:10 ratio), with earlier layers learning slower to preserve general features while later layers adapted more aggressively to task-specific patterns. Phase three (epochs 61--100) performed full fine-tuning at reduced learning rate (0.0001) with agricultural-specific augmentations enabled. This staged approach substantially outperformed end-to-end training from initialization.

\subsection{Explainability Mechanisms}\label{subsec:explainability}

\textbf{Grad-CAM++ Visual Explanations:} We employ Grad-CAM++ \cite{chattopadhay2018grad}, an enhanced version of Grad-CAM that provides improved localization accuracy through the use of second-order gradients. While standard Grad-CAM uses first-order gradients to weight feature map contributions, Grad-CAM++ computes pixel-wise weights using both first and second-order partial derivatives, enabling better handling of multiple instances of the same object class and more precise localization of disease symptoms within complex agricultural imagery.

The Grad-CAM++ process targets the final stage of ConvNeXt V2-Tiny, extracting feature maps and computing weighted combinations based on second-order gradient information. This approach produces finer-grained attention maps that more accurately highlight disease-affected regions, particularly valuable for diseases presenting multiple lesions or diffuse symptom patterns. ReLU activation eliminates negative contributions, focusing attention on features supporting the prediction. The resulting heatmap is upsampled to match input image dimensions and overlaid for visualization using jet or viridis colormaps based on user preference testing.

As an alternative, we also evaluated LayerCAM \cite{jiang2021layercam}, which generates explanations from multiple network layers and combines them for more comprehensive spatial coverage. While LayerCAM showed advantages for detecting early-stage diseases with subtle symptoms, Grad-CAM++ demonstrated superior performance for well-developed disease manifestations and was selected as the primary explanation method.

\textbf{Monte Carlo Dropout for Uncertainty Quantification:} Unlike standard inference where dropout is disabled, we maintain active dropout and perform 30 stochastic forward passes with different dropout patterns. Prediction variance across runs quantifies model uncertainty: high agreement indicates confidence, high variation indicates uncertainty. This approach enables decomposition of uncertainty into aleatoric components (inherent data noise) and epistemic components (model knowledge gaps). Epistemic uncertainty is particularly informative as it indicates potential benefit from additional training data. Temperature scaling calibrates raw uncertainty estimates to align with empirical accuracy.

\textbf{LLM-Based Natural Language Generation:} Unlike traditional template-based approaches, we employ Phi-3-mini \cite{abdin2024phi3}, a compact large language model (3.8B parameters), for generating context-aware and audience-adaptive explanations. This LLM-based approach enables more natural, flexible language generation that can capture the full spectrum of disease manifestations and contextual nuances that rigid templates cannot address.

Phi-3-mini receives structured inputs including the predicted disease class, confidence scores, uncertainty estimates, detected visual features from Grad-CAM++ analysis, and the target audience specification (farmer vs. extension officer). The model generates explanations tailored to the diagnostic scenario and user expertise level. Farmer-oriented explanations prioritize clarity and actionability (e.g., ``This appears to be Leaf Blast with 91\% confidence. Notice the gray oval spots with brown edges on the leaves---these are characteristic of this fungal disease. Consider applying fungicide within the next 2-3 days.''). Extension officer explanations provide technical depth with differential diagnosis considerations (e.g., ``Primary diagnosis: Leaf Blast (91\% confidence). Differential considerations: Brown Spot (5\%), early Bacterial Leaf Blight (3\%). Lesion morphology shows characteristic diamond-shaped progression. Microscopic confirmation recommended if edge necrosis pattern is atypical.'').

For mobile deployment, we utilize 4-bit quantization (GGUF format), reducing the model size from approximately 7.6GB to 2.1GB while maintaining explanation quality. This quantized model achieves inference times of 1.2--2.0 seconds on modern smartphones, enabling practical field deployment without cloud connectivity requirements.

\subsection{Training Methodology and Optimization}\label{subsec:training}

\textbf{Data Augmentation:} Extensive data augmentation enhanced model robustness to real-world variability. Geometric transformations included random rotations ($\pm15$ degrees), horizontal and vertical flips (50\% probability), random crops at scales 0.8--1.0, and elastic and perspective warping. Photometric augmentations comprised brightness variation ($\pm20\%$), contrast adjustment ($\pm25\%$), hue shift ($\pm10$ degrees), saturation modification ($\pm15\%$), and Gaussian noise injection ($\sigma = 0.01$). Domain-specific augmentations proved particularly valuable: simulated water droplets on leaves (prevalent in paddy field conditions), synthetic shadows, wind-induced blur effects, and depth-of-field variations. Mixup augmentation \cite{zhang2018mixup} was evaluated but did not improve performance for this classification task.

\textbf{Loss Function:} The loss function extends beyond standard classification to incorporate explainability constraints:
\begin{equation}
L_{total} = \lambda_{ce} \times L_{ce} + \lambda_{att} \times L_{attention} + \lambda_{cal} \times L_{calibration} + \lambda_{div} \times L_{diversity}
\end{equation}

The primary component is categorical cross-entropy ($\lambda_{ce} = 1.0$) with inverse frequency weighting ($w_i = N/(C \times n_i)$) to address class imbalance. Three additional terms were incorporated: attention consistency loss ($\lambda_{att} = 0.1$) maintains Grad-CAM explanation stability across similar inputs, calibration loss ($\lambda_{cal} = 0.05$) encourages well-calibrated uncertainty estimates, and diversity loss ($\lambda_{div} = 0.02$) prevents excessive reliance on limited feature subsets.

\textbf{Optimization Protocol:} Training employed AdamW with weight decay of 0.01 and cosine annealing learning rate schedule (0.001 to 0.0001). Batch size was set to 16 based on GPU memory constraints and training stability considerations. Gradient clipping (max norm 1.0) prevented gradient explosion. Early stopping terminated training if validation loss did not improve for 15 consecutive epochs. Convergence typically occurred between epochs 80--100, requiring approximately 6--8 hours on an RTX 3090. Hyperparameter optimization utilized Optuna for Bayesian search over learning rates, loss weights, dropout rates, and augmentation intensities.

\subsection{Experimental Design and Validation Strategy}\label{subsec:experimental}

The evaluation strategy addressed multiple objectives: classification accuracy and explanation quality, user study assessment of explanation utility and trustworthiness, and comparative analysis against baseline approaches.

The dataset was partitioned using stratified sampling to maintain consistent class distribution. The split allocated 70\% for training (7,285 images), 15\% for validation (1,561 images), and 15\% for testing (1,561 images). Stratification prevented potential bias toward specific disease categories in the evaluation sets.

Additionally, 5-fold cross-validation was performed using stratified sampling for each fold. This approach yielded five independent experiments, providing confidence intervals for all metrics and enabling statistical significance testing of performance differences.

Temporal robustness was also evaluated by reserving 20\% of images from each disease class as a temporal validation set. This configuration simulates deployment scenarios where the model encounters different disease presentations or environmental conditions than those observed during training.

Performance evaluation employed standard classification metrics: accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score, computed both per-class and macro-averaged. Top-2 and top-3 accuracy were also calculated, as practical deployment scenarios often benefit from presenting multiple diagnostic possibilities, particularly when disease co-occurrence is possible.

Explanation quality evaluation requires more sophisticated approaches than standard accuracy metrics. For Grad-CAM++ heatmaps, agricultural experts manually annotated disease symptom locations, enabling Intersection over Union (IoU) calculation to assess heatmap alignment. Insertion and deletion scores were computed to verify whether highlighted regions genuinely influence predictions. Point-game accuracy measured whether heatmaps correctly identify disease locations marked by experts.

Uncertainty quantification evaluation focused on calibration metrics: Expected Calibration Error (ECE), Maximum Calibration Error (MCE), and reliability diagrams. Well-calibrated models produce confidence scores that match empirical accuracy rates. Brier scores were also computed, combining both discrimination (class separability) and calibration (confidence accuracy) into a single measure.

Natural language explanation quality was assessed using BLEU and ROUGE scores, comparing generated text against expert-written reference explanations. Readability was evaluated using Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level and Automated Readability Index (ARI) to ensure appropriate language complexity for the target audience.

Comparative analysis included several baselines: standard ConvNeXt V2-Tiny without explainability features, ConvNeXt V2-Tiny with Grad-CAM++ only, ConvNeXt V2-Tiny with uncertainty quantification only, alternative architectures (ResNet50, VGG16, Inception-v3, EfficientNet-B0), and traditional machine learning methods (SVM and Random Forest with handcrafted features) to quantify deep learning improvements.

Ablation studies evaluated component contributions. Comparison of explanation methods revealed Grad-CAM++'s superior performance, though LIME and SHAP demonstrated advantages in specific scenarios. Monte Carlo Dropout outperformed ensemble methods for uncertainty quantification in computational efficiency, while ensembles showed marginal accuracy gains insufficient to justify practical deployment constraints. Systematic evaluation of data augmentation combinations, loss function components, and natural language generation strategies informed final design decisions.

\subsection{User Study Design and Evaluation}\label{subsec:user-study}

The user study evaluated system effectiveness with agricultural stakeholders in realistic decision-making contexts. Twelve participants were recruited across three expertise categories: agricultural extension officers with formal plant pathology training (n=4), experienced farmers with over 10 years of rice cultivation experience (n=4), and agricultural science students representing novice users (n=4).

Recruitment was conducted through partnerships with local agricultural departments, farmer cooperatives, and the university's agricultural science programs. While the sample size is modest, it provides balanced representation across expertise levels for preliminary evaluation.

The study collected demographic information including age, education level, years of agricultural experience, technology familiarity, and prior exposure to AI-based diagnostic tools. These variables enabled analysis of how user characteristics influence system interaction and trust formation.

A within-subjects experimental design was employed, with each participant evaluating three system configurations: a prediction-only baseline, a system augmented with Grad-CAM++ visual explanations, and the complete system incorporating visual explanations, uncertainty indicators, and natural language descriptions. Presentation order was counterbalanced across participants to control for learning effects.

Each participant evaluated 20 paddy disease images spanning different disease types and severity levels. For each image, participants first provided an independent diagnosis with confidence rating, then utilized the assigned system configuration to revise their assessment if desired. They subsequently rated system trustworthiness, explanation utility, and likelihood of field adoption using structured scales.

Sessions lasted approximately 60-90 minutes, including setup and post-task interviews. Individual sessions ensured independent responses and facilitated candid feedback.

Semi-structured interviews followed the task-based evaluation, covering interface usability, explanation comprehensibility, trust factors, comparison with existing diagnostic practices, perceived strengths and limitations, and desired enhancements. These interviews were audio-recorded with participant consent and transcribed for thematic analysis, providing qualitative insights to complement quantitative performance metrics.

\subsection{Computational Environment and Infrastructure}\label{subsec:computation}

Development and training were conducted on a deep learning workstation equipped with an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU (24GB VRAM) and CUDA 11.8 support, enabling ConvNeXt V2-Tiny training with batch sizes up to 32. The CPU (Intel Core i9-10900K with 32GB RAM) handled data preprocessing and analysis tasks.

The software environment comprised PyTorch 1.13.0 with torchvision 0.14.0 for deep learning, scikit-learn 1.1.0 for traditional machine learning baselines, and OpenCV 4.6.0 for image processing. Experiment tracking and hyperparameter optimization utilized Weights \& Biases (wandb) for systematic monitoring of training progress.

Deployment testing spanned multiple hardware configurations to evaluate realistic deployment scenarios: an NVIDIA Jetson Xavier NX for edge computing evaluation, various smartphones (Android and iOS) for mobile deployment testing, and standard laptops for user study sessions. This multi-platform testing provided comprehensive validation of system performance across diverse computational environments.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Classification Performance Analysis}\label{subsec:classification-performance}

Classification performance on the test set achieved 91.3\% accuracy overall, with precision at 90.7\%, recall at 90.2\%, and F1-score at 90.4\%. These results should be interpreted in context: reported accuracies exceeding 95\% typically derive from controlled laboratory datasets with optimal imaging conditions. The Paddy Doctor dataset presents realistic field conditions including water droplets, variable angles, and inconsistent lighting, making 91.3\% accuracy a strong result for practical deployment. The improvement over previous ResNet50-based approaches (85.2\% accuracy) demonstrates the effectiveness of modern CNN architectures for agricultural disease detection.

Five-fold cross-validation confirmed model stability, yielding mean accuracy of $90.8\% \pm 1.1\%$. This narrow standard deviation indicates that the model learns generalizable patterns rather than dataset-specific artifacts.

Top-k accuracy analysis reveals practical utility for clinical decision support. While top-1 accuracy reached 91.3\%, top-2 accuracy increased to 96.8\% and top-3 accuracy to 98.4\%. This indicates that even when the primary prediction is incorrect, the correct diagnosis typically appears among the top three candidates---enabling presentation of multiple possibilities for expert adjudication.

Per-class analysis revealed instructive variation. The Healthy class achieved perfect classification (100\% precision, recall, and F1), which is critical because false positives lead to unnecessary pesticide application with associated economic and environmental costs.

Leaf Blast achieved the highest disease classification performance (precision: 92.1\%, recall: 90.8\%, F1: 91.4\%), attributable to its distinctive elliptical lesions with gray centers and brown edges.

Brown Spot performed similarly well (precision: 89.3\%, recall: 87.6\%, F1: 88.4\%), with its characteristic concentric ring patterns providing consistent visual signatures across disease stages.

Bacterial Leaf Blight presented greater difficulty (precision: 86.7\%, recall: 84.2\%, F1: 85.4\%), as elongated lesions with wavy edges exhibit higher morphological variability.

The primary classification challenge involved differentiating Bacterial Leaf Blight from Bacterial Leaf Streak, with 12.3\% bidirectional confusion. Both diseases present as elongated lesions along leaf edges with subtle distinguishing features (lesion width, margin characteristics) that challenge even expert diagnosticians---our annotation validators exhibited disagreement on several such cases.

Tungro virus detection achieved moderate performance (precision: 81.4\%, recall: 79.8\%, F1: 80.6\%), reflecting the complex symptomatology that can vary significantly based on plant age, virus strain, and environmental conditions. The orange-yellow discoloration patterns provide less consistent visual signatures compared to lesion-based diseases.

Comprehensive confusion matrix analysis reveals systematic error patterns that provide insights into model behavior and potential improvement directions. The confusion matrix (Figure~\ref{fig:confusion-matrix}) demonstrates strong diagonal concentration, indicating generally accurate classification, with off-diagonal elements revealing specific inter-class confusion patterns.

The most significant confusion occurs between morphologically similar disease pairs: Bacterial Leaf Blight vs. Bacterial Leaf Streak (8.7\% bidirectional confusion), Brown Spot vs. Narrow Brown Leaf Spot (6.4\% confusion), and early-stage Hispa vs. Tungro (5.2\% confusion). These confusion patterns align with known diagnostic challenges in plant pathology, where expert human diagnosticians also experience difficulty differentiating these disease pairs without additional contextual information.

Interestingly, confusion patterns exhibit asymmetry in several cases, suggesting systematic bias in model decision-making. For instance, Bacterial Leaf Streak is more frequently misclassified as Bacterial Leaf Blight (11.2%) than the reverse (6.3%), potentially indicating that the model learns more distinctive features for Bacterial Leaf Blight due to its higher prevalence in the training dataset.

Severity-based analysis reveals that classification accuracy decreases for early-stage diseases (accuracy: 78.4\%) compared to advanced symptoms (accuracy: 91.7\%). This pattern suggests that while the model performs excellently for well-developed disease manifestations, early detection—crucial for effective treatment—remains challenging and requires continued research attention.

\subsection{Explainability Analysis}\label{subsec:explainability-analysis}

Grad-CAM++ explanation quality demonstrates strong alignment with expert knowledge and agricultural relevance. Quantitative evaluation using IoU metrics with expert-annotated disease regions achieved a mean IoU of $0.72 \pm 0.10$ across all disease categories. This performance indicates that visual explanations successfully highlight disease-relevant regions while avoiding irrelevant background areas.

The insertion score analysis, measuring performance changes when highlighted regions are emphasized, achieved a mean score of 0.76, indicating that regions identified as important by Grad-CAM++ genuinely contribute to model predictions. Conversely, deletion score analysis (0.18) confirms that removing highlighted regions significantly degrades model performance, validating explanation faithfulness.

Point-game accuracy assessment, evaluating whether explanation heatmaps correctly identify specific disease locations marked by agricultural experts, achieved 81.4\% success rate. This metric provides direct validation that explanations point to actual disease symptoms rather than spurious correlations or background features.

Disease-specific analysis reveals variation in explanation quality across different pathological manifestations. Lesion-based diseases (Leaf Blast, Brown Spot, Bacterial Leaf Blight) achieve higher explanation quality (mean IoU: 0.74) compared to systemic diseases with diffuse symptoms (Tungro, Downy Mildew) (mean IoU: 0.58). This pattern reflects the inherent explainability advantages of diseases with localized, visually distinct symptoms.

Monte Carlo Dropout uncertainty quantification demonstrates well-calibrated confidence estimates that correlate appropriately with prediction accuracy. Expected Calibration Error (ECE) analysis yielded 0.087, indicating relatively good calibration where confidence scores reflect actual accuracy rates.

Reliability diagram analysis shows strong correlation between predicted confidence and actual accuracy across different confidence ranges. High-confidence predictions ($>0.9$) achieve 94.2\% accuracy, while low-confidence predictions ($<0.5$) achieve 67.8\% accuracy, providing meaningful discrimination for practical decision-making applications.

Uncertainty decomposition reveals that epistemic uncertainty (model uncertainty) accounts for approximately 60\% of total predictive uncertainty, while aleatoric uncertainty (inherent data noise) contributes 40\%. This decomposition suggests that model improvement through additional training data could substantially reduce uncertainty, providing guidance for future data collection efforts.

Disease-specific uncertainty analysis shows that morphologically complex diseases (Tungro, Bacterial diseases) generate higher uncertainty estimates compared to diseases with distinctive visual signatures (Leaf Blast, Brown Spot). This pattern indicates that uncertainty estimates appropriately reflect diagnostic difficulty and can guide users toward cases requiring expert consultation.

Natural language explanation quality assessment employs both automated metrics and expert evaluation to ensure clarity and agricultural relevance. BLEU score comparison with expert-written reference explanations achieved 0.73, indicating strong semantic similarity while maintaining natural language fluency.

Readability analysis using Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level assessment yielded scores of 8.2 for farmer-oriented explanations and 11.4 for extension officer explanations, appropriately matching target audience literacy levels. These scores ensure explanations remain accessible while providing necessary technical detail for different user categories.

Expert evaluation by three agricultural specialists assessed explanation accuracy, relevance, and clarity across 100 generated explanations. Overall scores averaged 4.2/5.0 for accuracy, 4.0/5.0 for relevance, and 4.1/5.0 for clarity, indicating strong performance across evaluation criteria. Specific feedback highlighted the value of connecting visual explanations with textual descriptions and providing uncertainty indicators alongside diagnostic statements.

\subsection{Comparative Analysis with Baseline Methods}\label{subsec:comparative}

Comprehensive comparison with alternative CNN architectures reveals the effectiveness of the ConvNeXt V2-Tiny-based approach for agricultural disease detection. VGG16 achieved 79.3\% accuracy while requiring significantly more parameters (138M vs. 28.6M), demonstrating inferior efficiency. ResNet50 achieved 85.2\% accuracy with comparable parameters (25.6M) but lower overall performance. EfficientNet-B0 achieved moderate accuracy (83.7\%) with fewer parameters (5.3M) but showed reduced explanation quality due to architectural differences affecting Grad-CAM++ generation.

Inception-v3 demonstrated strong classification performance (86.1\% accuracy) but exhibited longer inference times (47ms vs. 23ms) and higher memory requirements that limit mobile deployment feasibility. The multi-scale feature processing in Inception-v3 provides some accuracy advantages but at computational costs that outweigh benefits for practical agricultural applications.

Traditional machine learning approaches using handcrafted features achieved substantially lower performance: SVM with HOG features (71.2\%), Random Forest with color and texture features (68.7\%), and k-NN with SIFT descriptors (64.3\%). These results confirm the superiority of deep learning approaches for complex pattern recognition tasks in agricultural imaging.

Systematic comparison of explanation methods (Table~\ref{tab:visual-explanation}) reveals trade-offs between different approaches to model interpretability. LIME achieved IoU of 0.61 with expert annotations but exhibited inconsistent explanations for visually similar inputs, limiting reliability for agricultural applications where consistency is crucial for user trust.

SHAP explanations demonstrated higher computational overhead (3.2s vs. 0.4s per explanation) while achieving comparable IoU (0.64) to Grad-CAM. The pixel-level attributions provided by SHAP offer detailed explanations but may overwhelm agricultural users who require clear, actionable insights rather than fine-grained technical details.

Vanilla CAM achieved lower explanation quality (IoU: 0.57) due to architectural limitations requiring global average pooling that reduces spatial resolution. The coarser explanations provided by CAM prove insufficient for identifying specific disease locations within complex agricultural imagery.

Integrated gradient explanations achieved high-quality attributions (IoU: 0.71) but required substantial computational resources (2.8s per explanation) that preclude real-time applications. While technically superior in some aspects, the computational requirements limit practical deployment in resource-constrained agricultural environments.

\subsection{User Study Results and Human-AI Interaction Analysis}\label{subsec:user-study-results}

The user study included 12 participants evenly distributed across three expertise categories: agricultural extension officers (n=4, mean experience: 9.2 years), experienced farmers (n=4, mean experience: 17.5 years of rice cultivation), and final-year agricultural science students (n=4).

Baseline diagnostic performance without AI assistance varied significantly across user categories. Extension officers achieved 73.1\% accuracy on the 20-image diagnostic task, farmers achieved 67.5\% accuracy, and students achieved 52.3\% accuracy. These baseline measurements provide context for evaluating the value of AI-assisted diagnosis across different expertise levels.

Confidence calibration analysis revealed that all user categories exhibited overconfidence bias, with stated confidence levels consistently exceeding actual accuracy. Extension officers showed the best calibration (9.2\% confidence-accuracy gap), followed by farmers (13.8\% gap) and students (19.4\% gap). This pattern indicates that uncertainty indicators provide particular value for less experienced users who tend toward overconfidence.

Systematic evaluation across three system configurations revealed differential impacts across user categories. The prediction-only system improved accuracy for all user groups: +11.3\% for extension officers (reaching 84.4\%), +14.7\% for farmers (reaching 82.2\%), and +21.5\% for students (reaching 73.8\%). These improvements demonstrate substantial value of AI assistance even without explainability features.

Addition of visual explanations (Grad-CAM++ heatmaps) provided further improvements: extension officers gained an additional 3.6\% (reaching 88.0\%), farmers gained 5.2\% (reaching 87.4\%), and students gained 6.1\% (reaching 79.9\%). Qualitative feedback indicated that visual explanations helped users identify disease symptoms they had overlooked and increased confidence in AI recommendations when highlighted regions aligned with their visual assessment.

The complete system including uncertainty indicators and natural language explanations yielded the highest performance: extension officers reached 90.5\% (+2.5\%), farmers reached 90.8\% (+3.4\%), and students reached 84.7\% (+4.8\%). While these incremental improvements were smaller in magnitude, qualitative feedback emphasized the value of uncertainty communication for supporting decisions in ambiguous cases. Figure~\ref{fig:user-study-results} summarizes the impact of different explanation modalities on user performance across expertise levels, while Figure~\ref{fig:explanation-effectiveness} illustrates the complementary nature of multi-level explanations for various agricultural decision-making scenarios. The integrated user interface (Figure~\ref{fig:user-interface}) demonstrates how visual explanations, natural language descriptions, and uncertainty indicators are presented cohesively to support farmer decision-making.

Trust assessment employed Likert scale ratings (1-5) across multiple dimensions: system reliability, explanation clarity, recommendation confidence, and likelihood of adoption. Overall trust scores averaged 4.1/5.0 across all participants, with variation across user categories: extension officers scored highest (4.3/5.0), followed by students (4.1/5.0) and farmers (3.8/5.0).

The complete explanation system generated significantly higher trust scores compared to prediction-only systems ($p < 0.01$, Wilcoxon signed-rank test), indicating that explainability features contribute meaningfully to user acceptance. Trust improvements were most pronounced for explanation clarity and recommendation confidence dimensions.

Correlation analysis revealed that trust scores correlated positively with system accuracy (Spearman's $\rho = 0.72$, $p < 0.01$) and negatively with displayed uncertainty levels ($\rho = -0.58$, $p < 0.05$). This pattern indicates that users appropriately adjusted their trust based on system performance indicators, supporting the value of uncertainty communication for calibrated human-AI interaction.

Thematic analysis of semi-structured interviews identified key themes related to system usability, explanation effectiveness, and deployment considerations. The most frequently mentioned positive aspects included visual explanation clarity (mentioned by 11/12 participants), diagnostic accuracy improvements (10/12), and uncertainty communication value (8/12).

Concerns and limitations highlighted by participants included occasional explanation inconsistency (mentioned by 5/12 participants), desire for additional contextual information such as treatment recommendations (7/12), and questions about system performance under varying field conditions (6/12). These insights provide guidance for future system development and deployment strategies.

User category differences emerged in qualitative feedback. Extension officers emphasized the value of uncertainty indicators for risk assessment and the importance of explanation consistency for maintaining professional credibility. Farmers prioritized visual explanation clarity and expressed interest in integration with treatment recommendation systems. Students appreciated the educational value of explanations for developing disease identification skills.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The 91.3\% classification accuracy represents a significant achievement for explainable agricultural AI. This performance approaches the $>95\%$ figures reported for black-box models on highly controlled datasets while maintaining full explainability---demonstrating that the traditional trade-off between performance and interpretability can be substantially mitigated with modern architectures. The adoption of ConvNeXt V2-Tiny, with its depthwise convolutions and modern normalization techniques, contributed approximately 6\% accuracy improvement over previous ResNet50-based approaches. Importantly, the system substantially exceeds human expert baselines: extension officers in our study achieved 73.1\% baseline accuracy, while farmers achieved 67.5\%. The cross-validation stability ($90.8\% \pm 1.1\%$) indicates that the model learns generalizable features rather than memorizing dataset-specific artifacts.

Visual explanation quality, measured by IoU of $0.72 \pm 0.10$ with expert annotations, confirms that Grad-CAM++ successfully identifies agriculturally relevant disease regions with improved localization compared to standard Grad-CAM. The second-order gradient computation in Grad-CAM++ provides finer-grained attention maps particularly beneficial for diseases presenting multiple lesions. Disease-specific analysis revealed that lesion-based diseases with distinct morphological features (Leaf Blast, Brown Spot) achieved higher IoU scores (0.78), while systemic diseases with diffuse symptoms scored lower (0.62). This pattern suggests that explanation quality benchmarks should account for disease-specific characteristics. The explanation faithfulness metrics (insertion: 0.76, deletion: 0.18) confirm that visual explanations genuinely reflect model reasoning rather than serving as post-hoc rationalizations. Uncertainty quantification demonstrated reasonable calibration (ECE: 0.087), producing confidence estimates that meaningfully support decision-making.

The user study revealed substantial differences across stakeholder categories. Agricultural students showed the largest performance improvements (+32.4\% with the complete system), suggesting that explainable AI systems serve valuable educational functions beyond diagnostic assistance. Extension officers showed more modest accuracy improvements (+17.4\%) but emphasized the value of uncertainty indicators for risk assessment and decision documentation---aspects critical to their professional responsibilities. Farmers achieved substantial improvements (+23.3\%) and expressed strong preferences for visual explanations paired with practical treatment recommendations. Trust scores correlated positively with accuracy (Spearman's $\rho = 0.72$) and negatively with uncertainty ($\rho = -0.58$), indicating that users appropriately calibrated their trust based on system performance indicators.

From a practical deployment perspective, computational requirements are favorable: 28ms inference time for ConvNeXt V2-Tiny, 28.6M parameters, and approximately 115MB storage for the classification model enable mobile deployment. The 4-bit quantized Phi-3-mini model adds approximately 2.1GB storage but enables fully offline LLM-based explanation generation. Explanation generation overhead (0.5s for Grad-CAM++, 0.8s for uncertainty quantification, 1.2--2.0s for natural language generation) introduces noticeable latency but remains acceptable for practical use. Battery consumption of 6--10\% for 10--20 daily diagnoses appears acceptable for field use, though device-dependent variation is expected. The modular architecture facilitates integration with existing agricultural information systems, IoT sensors, and precision agriculture platforms.

\textbf{Limitations:} Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the evaluation relies entirely on the Paddy Doctor dataset from specific geographic regions, which may limit generalizability to other rice-growing areas with different environmental conditions, cultivars, or disease presentations. Second, the user study sample (n=12), while providing valuable preliminary insights, remains too small for definitive conclusions; expanded studies with more participants across diverse settings are needed to validate these findings. Third, while Phi-3-mini provides more flexible natural language generation than template-based approaches, occasional hallucinations or overly confident language may occur; careful prompt engineering and output validation remain necessary. Fourth, Monte Carlo Dropout provides computationally efficient uncertainty estimates but may not capture all relevant sources of uncertainty, particularly those related to out-of-distribution inputs. Fifth, the 4-bit quantized LLM requires approximately 2.1GB additional storage, which may be prohibitive for some low-end devices. Finally, the focus on single-image diagnosis may not adequately address real-world scenarios where farmers submit multiple images of the same plant or field for more comprehensive assessment.

\textbf{Future Directions:} Several promising directions warrant further investigation. More advanced explanation techniques—particularly counterfactual and contrastive approaches specifically tailored for agricultural contexts—could provide more intuitive insights than current attention-based methods. Integration of multi-modal sensing (hyperspectral imaging, thermal cameras) could significantly enhance diagnostic accuracy, especially for diseases with subtle visual symptoms. Continual learning capabilities would allow the system to adapt to emerging disease variants and changing environmental conditions without requiring complete retraining. Federated learning approaches could enable collaborative model improvement across multiple farms and regions while preserving data privacy—a consideration that may prove crucial for adoption. Beyond paddy, extension to other crop types represents an obvious next step, as does integration with precision agriculture technologies for automated early warning systems. Multi-language support will be essential for reaching farmers in diverse linguistic regions, and integration with agricultural insurance systems could provide additional incentives for adoption while generating valuable data for risk assessment.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

This research presents ExplainableCropAI, a comprehensive framework that addresses a critical gap in agricultural AI: the tension between performance and explainability. By systematically integrating multiple explainable AI techniques---Grad-CAM++ for visual explanations with improved localization, Monte Carlo Dropout for uncertainty quantification, and LLM-based natural language generation using Phi-3-mini for context-aware explanations---we demonstrate that transparency and performance can be effectively balanced to create trustworthy agricultural decision support systems.

The system achieves 91.3\% classification accuracy while maintaining full explainability, substantially exceeding human expert baselines observed in our user study (extension officers: 73.1\%, farmers: 67.5\%). The adoption of ConvNeXt V2-Tiny, a modern CNN architecture with depthwise convolutions and GELU activations, provided approximately 6\% accuracy improvement over previous ResNet50-based approaches. Cross-validation stability ($90.8\% \pm 1.1\%$) indicates reliable performance that generalizes across diverse conditions. Visual explanations using Grad-CAM++ align well with expert knowledge (IoU: 0.72), uncertainty quantification produces well-calibrated confidence estimates (ECE: 0.087), and LLM-based natural language explanations demonstrate both clarity and relevance (BLEU: 0.73).

The user study demonstrated substantial improvements across all expertise levels: agricultural students improved by 32.4\%, farmers by 23.3\%, and extension officers by 17.4\%. Explainability features contributed meaningfully to user acceptance, with the complete explanation system generating significantly higher trust scores compared to prediction-only alternatives ($p < 0.01$). Computational requirements remain practical for mobile deployment (28ms inference, 28.6M parameters for ConvNeXt V2-Tiny, approximately 115MB for the classification model plus 2.1GB for the quantized Phi-3-mini LLM).

This work makes several key contributions. First, we demonstrate empirically that comprehensive explainability can be integrated with agricultural AI without prohibitive performance penalties---a concern that has deterred some practitioners. Second, we provide evidence that multi-modal explanations genuinely improve both user performance and trust across diverse stakeholder categories, not only among highly technical users. Third, we present a working technical framework that balances accuracy, explainability, and computational efficiency in deployable form. Fourth, our evaluation methodology encompasses both technical performance metrics and crucial human factors often overlooked in computer vision research.

Several priorities emerge for future work. Field validation under realistic deployment conditions remains essential to confirm laboratory findings. Extension to diverse crop types and disease categories would expand the system's utility. Larger-scale user studies involving extended field deployment will provide more definitive evidence of real-world impact. Integration with precision agriculture technologies could create synergies enhancing overall agricultural management. As climate change intensifies disease pressures and food security challenges demand increasingly efficient practices, trustworthy AI systems that augment rather than replace human expertise become ever more critical. This research provides both technical foundations and empirical encouragement for developing such systems---systems that contribute to sustainable agricultural development while preserving human agency in decisions that shape food systems and livelihoods.
