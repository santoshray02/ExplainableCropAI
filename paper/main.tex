%% Abstract

\abstract{Deep learning models have demonstrated strong feasibility for crop disease diagnosis in terms of precision; however, their black-box nature limits practical usage. Farmers and agricultural professionals need to understand an AI diagnosis before they can act on it in decision making. In this study, we propose ExplainableCropAI, a system that unites technical performance and real-world applicability. The framework integrates three complementary explainability techniques: Grad-CAM++, a visual heatmap for enhancing localization performance; Monte Carlo Dropout, an uncertainty quantification technique; and LLM-based natural language generation with Phi-3-mini, which produces context-aware, audience-adaptive explanations. The design treats crop diagnosis as a human-centred visual decision-support problem, coupling visual evidence localisation with an uncertainty-aware visual interface and language-based explanation. It was fully explainable and achieved 97.5\% accuracy on a Paddy Doctor dataset of 10,407 photos spanning 10 classes (nine diseases and healthy), using a ConvNeXt V2-Tiny model, an up-to-date CNN design with depthwise convolutions, LayerNorm and GELU activations. The system couples calibrated uncertainty estimates with faithful visual attributions and grounded language explanations, evaluated with automatic, reproducible metrics. This highlights the potential to balance the accuracy of categorization with the interpretability of the model in agricultural AI systems.}

\keywords{Visual decision support, Human-centred visual computing, Visual evidence localisation, ConvNeXt V2-Tiny model, Explainable artificial intelligence, Grad-CAM++, Paddy disease detection, Uncertainty quantification, Visual explanations}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Rice is the staple food for roughly 3.5 billion people across Asia, Africa and Europe, is grown on about 167 million hectares worldwide, and supports the livelihoods of some 144 million farming households \cite{kamilaris2018deep}. Disease is a leading threat to that food base: each year, infections and the crop failures they cause remove an estimated 125--180 million tonnes of rice and impose heavy costs on farming economies \cite{savary2019global,rahman2020deep}. Diagnosis is hard because the crop is attacked by more than 70 pathogens, many of which look alike in their early stages. Field identification is consequently unreliable---reported agreement among human assessors is only 23--47\% and shifts with the assessor's experience and the surrounding conditions \cite{bock2021severity,nutter1993assessing}. The scale of the loss is large: the Food and Agriculture Organization attributes 20--40\% of annual global crop losses---around US\$220 billion---to pests and disease \cite{fao2020planthealth,fao2021pests}. Subjective severity scoring is only moderately consistent, with intraclass correlations between 0.60 and 0.85 \cite{mrisho2020accuracy,oerke2020sensor}. Worse, the lag from first symptom through sampling to expert confirmation often exceeds the window in which a fast-moving infection can still be contained \cite{ristaino2021persistent}. Automated, scalable diagnosis is therefore needed.

Deep learning has reshaped how these diseases are diagnosed. On benchmark plant-disease imagery, convolutional networks now exceed 95\% accuracy \cite{mohanty2016using}---a large jump from the 60--75\% of early-2000s hand-crafted image processing and the 80--85\% of 2010s feature-based machine learning \cite{liakos2018machine,shafay2025review,akter2024riceleafcnn}. These numbers, though, are largely measured on curated images under controlled capture. Moved to working fields, the same models typically lose 15--22\% accuracy relative to their curated benchmark \cite{mrisho2020accuracy}. Among standard backbones, ResNet variants reach 92--96\% across crops at modest compute \cite{he2016deep,ferentinos2018deep}, and single-stage detectors such as YOLO support real-time field use \cite{fuentes2017robust}---including our own earlier YOLOv8 detector on this same Paddy Doctor dataset \cite{kumar2026yolo}. The backbone we adopt, ConvNeXt V2 \cite{woo2023convnext,wang2024wheatconvnext}, is a 2023 pure-CNN design that folds transformer-era ideas---depthwise convolutions, LayerNorm and GELU activations---into a convolutional network and attains state-of-the-art accuracy. 
Rice cultivation poses distinctive imaging challenges: flooded fields, high humidity, and a dense canopy with complex lighting and occlusion. Using CNN customization and paddy specific augmentation procedures, Rahman et al. \cite{rahman2020deep} achieved an accuracy of 94.33\%. Transfer learning may be used to reduce the training time and training data, which is done by leveraging the ImageNet data \cite{lu2017identification}.



Although technology is quickly advancing, AI has not been widely adopted in agriculture, partly due to trust difficulties \cite{wolfert2017big,ahmad2024nextgen,gardezi2024aitrust}. Farm decisions carry a large financial impact: a false diagnosis leads to inappropriate pesticide use, crop damage, and lost income. Earning farmers' trust is therefore essential. To solve these problems, there are numerous XAI methods: For the predictions \cite{selvaraju2017grad}, Grad-CAM generates heat maps for the pertinent sections of the image. Grad-CAM++ \cite{chattopadhay2018grad,khan2024potato} developed second-order gradient weighting to improve localization accuracy, and Grad-CAM-based attribution has been deployed on edge devices for real-time crop disease diagnosis \cite{karim2024grapegradcam}. SHAP provides explanations in an intuitive game-theoretic format that supports pixel-level explanations but has high computing costs \cite{lundberg2017unified}; recent work unifies class-activation mapping with Shapley-value attribution for more faithful, theoretically grounded localization \cite{cai2025shapleycam}. LIME, by contrast, provides explanations by perturbing the input \cite{ribeiro2016should} and has low computational costs.

But visual explanations alone are not enough; a confidence estimate is also needed. Uncertainty quantification is performed with several inference passes with active dropout \cite{gal2016dropout}, which may be decomposed into aleatoric (data noise) and epistemic (model knowledge) components \cite{kendall2017uncertainties} via Monte Carlo Dropout. This information of uncertainty is useful in making proper treatment selections. The other is natural language explanations. Template-based solutions produce organized output but lack the flexibility required for complex diagnostic circumstances. They can provide context-aware and audience-adaptive explanations, such as explaining the complete range of disease manifestations, as proven by Large Language Models (LLMs) \cite{abdin2024phi3,zhou2025llmcrop,singh2024llmagri}. Visual-language foundation models have recently been applied to disease diagnosis and human--AI co-decision, underscoring the value of language-based interfaces for expert--user collaboration \cite{yao2025vlmdiagnosis}. Farmers adopt research advances when they trust them, they are culturally acceptable, and they are economically viable \cite{lee2004trust}. Explainability helps to mitigate the trust gap by enabling informed decision making.

In this study, an explainable paddy disease classification system is developed to overcome the trust gap between the applicability of AI and farmers' adoption. Whereas our prior work \cite{kumar2026yolo} optimised YOLOv8 for high-accuracy paddy disease \emph{detection}, the present work shifts the focus from raw detection accuracy to \emph{interpretable, uncertainty-aware} classification and decision support for agricultural stakeholders. The three main challenges are: (i) embedding multi-level explainability mechanisms into state-of-the-art CNN architectures, (ii) quantifying explanation faithfulness and uncertainty calibration with automatic, reproducible metrics, and (iii) optimising the system for edge deployment without compromising accuracy or explainability.

This work approaches crop disease diagnosis as a visual decision-support problem, emphasising visual evidence localisation, uncertainty-aware visual interfaces, and the human-centred interpretation of image-based models. Our specific contributions are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Visual Evidence Localisation Benchmark}: Under identical settings, we compare six attribution methods (Grad-CAM, Grad-CAM++, LayerCAM, Integrated Gradients, LIME, and SHAP) using model-based faithfulness metrics (insertion/deletion), attribution stability, and runtime, yielding a reusable benchmark for visual-explanation quality in crop-disease imaging.

    \item \textbf{Multi-level Explanation System}: A novel framework that combines Grad-CAM++ for visual heatmaps with improved localization, Monte Carlo Dropout for uncertainty quantification (30 stochastic passes), and Phi-3-mini LLM (3.8B parameters) for context-aware natural language generation.

    \item \textbf{ConvNeXt V2-Tiny Integration}: We apply the ConvNeXt V2-Tiny architecture to paddy disease diagnosis with full support for gradient-based explanation methods (10,407 images, 10 disease classes, and 97.5\% accuracy).

    \item \textbf{On-Device Language Generation}: 4-bit (GGUF) quantisation shrinks the Phi-3-mini explanation model from 7.6\,GB to 2.1\,GB---a 73\% reduction---bringing natural-language diagnosis within the memory budget of a mid-range smartphone while preserving explanation quality.

    \item \textbf{Audience-Adaptive Generation}: LLM-based explanation system that tailors output complexity to user expertise level, providing actionable recommendations for farmers and technical depth for extension officers.
\end{itemize}

\section{Materials and Methods}\label{sec:methods}

The system comprises five interconnected components (Figure~\ref{fig:system-architecture}): 1. the ConvNeXt V2-Tiny model for disease classification, 2. the Grad-CAM++ module for generating visual explanations, 3. Monte Carlo Dropout for quantifying uncertainty, 4. a natural language generator (LLM) for textual explanations (Phi-3-mini), and 5. a recommendation engine for treatment suggestions. The modular structure allows for optimizing each module separately, while preserving system coherence.

A key design consideration was deployment flexibility, supporting multiple operation modes: full cloud computing, edge computing, and a hybrid distributed mode. Also the system provides various levels of explanation based on the competence of the user. Recommendations are straightforward and practical for the farmers and technical assessments are offered in detail for the extension staff. The visual heatmaps are accompanied by uncertainty scores and written explanations, thus constituting a holistic decision support system as depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:explanation-flowchart}. The integrated decision-support interface that presents these components to the user is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:user-interface}.

\subsection{Dataset and Preprocessing}\label{subsec:dataset}

Our experiments use the publicly available Paddy Doctor dataset \cite{petchiammal2023paddy}: 10,407 in-field photographs at $480 \times 640$ resolution, labelled across ten categories---nine paddy diseases plus a healthy class. The photographs were taken in working fields, under natural light and at several crop-growth stages, so they carry the symptom variability that a deployed system must tolerate. The class frequencies (Table~\ref{tab:dataset-distribution}) are uneven, ranging from Healthy at 16.9\% down to Bacterial Panicle Blight at 3.2\%---roughly a 5:1 ratio that broadly tracks natural disease incidence in rice. We hold these proportions fixed in every partition through stratified splitting; the resulting per-split counts are listed in Table~\ref{tab:split-counts}.

We use the dataset's provided expert labels as released, without additional re-annotation. The images were pre-processed by resizing to $640 \times 640$ to balance the cost of computation with the level of detail required for enhancement of subtle disease presentations, Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) to improve local contrast, color space transformations to optimally highlight disease-specific features, and normalization to ImageNet statistics for transfer learning.

\subsection{Deep Learning Architecture and Modifications}\label{subsec:dl-architecture}

We select ConvNeXt V2-Tiny as the backbone model, which is a modern design of CNN that incorporates several significant advances of transformer models while preserving the efficiency of CNN \cite{woo2023convnext}. The ConveXt V2 released in 2023 adopts depthwise separable convolutions that significantly reduce the computational costs, without reducing the representation power. The architecture also switches to using LayerNorm instead of BatchNorm for training stability and uses GELU (Gaussian Error Linear Unit) activations for smoother gradients than ReLU. ConvNeXt V2-Tiny has 27.9 million parameters and offers a compelling trade-off between model size and efficiency, making it ideal for mobile use. Most crucially, ConvNeXt V2 has a rigorously ordered hierarchical feature extraction structure, providing strong support for gradient-based feature-explanation methods such as Grad-CAM++. The attention mechanism of Vision Transformers produces explanations that are less obvious for agricultural stakeholders compared to saliency maps created by a CNN, despite comparable accuracy when evaluated on benchmark datasets. The depthwise convolution structure of ConvNeXt V2 creates localised feature responses that readily map to interpretable visual explanations, making it very suitable for our explainability aims.

We make minimal modifications to the standard ConvNeXt V2-Tiny: the classification head is replaced to predict 10 classes (9 diseases and healthy), and the global average pooling before the head is retained so that the last-stage feature maps remain available for Grad-CAM++. A dropout layer (rate 0.3) precedes the linear classifier; keeping this dropout active at inference enables Monte Carlo Dropout uncertainty estimation through repeated stochastic forward passes, with no additional architectural complexity. Forward and backward hooks on the last stage expose the activations and gradients that Grad-CAM++ requires.

The model is fine-tuned end-to-end from ImageNet-pretrained weights. We did not find staged freezing necessary: end-to-end fine-tuning of the pretrained backbone converged reliably on this dataset, with optimisation details given in Section~\ref{subsec:training}.

\subsection{Explainability Mechanism}\label{subsec:explainability}

\textbf{Grad-CAM++ Visual Explanations:} Our primary visual explainer is Grad-CAM++ \cite{chattopadhay2018grad}. The original Grad-CAM assigns each feature-map channel a single weight from a globally pooled first-order gradient. Grad-CAM++ instead derives a separate, spatially varying weight for every location from higher-order (first- and second-order) gradient terms. This sharpens the resulting heatmaps and copes with images in which the target symptom recurs across several disjoint regions---as lesion diseases often do---giving tighter localisation of the affected tissue in cluttered field photographs.

In the Grad-CAM++ process, feature maps are extracted from the last convolutional layer of ConvNeXt V2-Tiny, and second-order gradient information is calculated and used to determine the weights for the weighted combination of feature maps. Such method is able to generate more fine-grained attention maps that give more accurate highlight to disease-affected regions, especially useful when dealing with diseases that have multiple lesions or diffuse symptom patterns. ReLU activation throws away negative contributions and concentrates on features that aid the prediction. It produces a heatmap which is upsampled to a size appropriate to the input image, which is then overlaid for visualization, with the choice of jet or viridis colormaps being determined by user preference testing. Instead, it also tested LayerCAM \cite{jiang2021layercam} to produce explanations from a variety of network layers and fuse these layers for more extensive spatial coverage. LayerCAM proved to be beneficial with early stages of diseases with minor symptoms, meanwhile Grad-CAM++ was more effective with well developed disease symptoms and was chosen as the main explanation method.

\textbf{Monte Carlo Dropout for Uncertainty Quantification:} We use dropout during training and take 30 forward passes with different dropout patterns instead of the typical inference with dropout disabled. Model uncertainty is measured by prediction variance across runs where high agreement means high confidence and high variance means significant uncertainty; this approach has proven effective for uncertainty-aware plant disease diagnosis \cite{pugazhendi2026sugarcanemcdropout}. The uncertainty can thus be decomposed into data (intrinsic uncertainty) and model (extrinsic uncertainty). Epistemic uncertainty is particularly informative, as it indicates where additional training data would be most valuable. Temperature scaling \cite{guo2017calibration} calibrates the predicted confidences so they better match empirical accuracy.

\textbf{LLM-Based Natural Language Generation:} Instead of using templates, we use the compact Large Language Model (LLM) Phi-3-mini \cite{abdin2024phi3} to produce context-aware and audience-adaptive explanations. This LLM-based method allows more flexible and natural language generation, which can account for the various symptom clusters and situational nuances of disease that a fixed template can't handle.

Phi-3-mini takes structured input comprising the anticipated disease class, the confidence scores, the uncertainty estimates, the discovered visual features from the Grad-CAM++ analysis, and the target audience specification (farmer vs. extension officer). The model produces explanations depending on the diagnostic scenario and user knowledge level. Farmer-oriented explanations emphasize clarity and actionability (e.g., ``This looks like Leaf Blast with 91\% confidence. Notice the gray oval dots with brown margins on the leaves---this is typical of this fungal illness. Consider a fungicide spray within the next 2--3 days.''). Extension-officer explanations add technical depth, including differential diagnostic considerations (e.g., ``Primary diagnosis: Leaf Blast (91\% confidence); Brown Spot (5\%) and early Bacterial Leaf Blight (3\%) as differentials. Lesion morphology shows a distinctive diamond-shaped progression. Microscopic confirmation is advised if an uncommon edge-necrosis pattern is present.''). For mobile deployment, we employ 4-bit quantization (GGUF format) which reduces the model size from around 7.6GB to 2.1GB while preserving the quality of explanations. This quantized model provides 1.2-2.0 seconds inference time on contemporary smartphones enabling realistic field deployment without the need for cloud connectivity.

\textbf{Safety and grounding of recommendations.} Because inappropriate pesticide or treatment advice carries economic and environmental risk, the language model \emph{rephrases} rather than \emph{originates} agronomic content. All treatment, prevention, severity and chemical-class statements are supplied to the model as typed fields drawn from a curated knowledge base compiled from public agricultural-extension references (e.g., the IRRI Rice Knowledge Bank and state agricultural-university extension material); the model is constrained to these fields and does not generate free-form dosages. A template-based generator reproduces the same knowledge-base content verbatim whenever the LLM is unavailable or fails, so the system never relies on unconstrained generation for actionable advice. The system also applies confidence- and agreement-based thresholds: high confidence with high inter-pass agreement yields a direct diagnosis; moderate values add explicit verification-advised wording; and low confidence or high uncertainty triggers a ``consult an agricultural expert'' referral with no firm treatment asserted. ExplainableCropAI is therefore positioned as decision \emph{support} rather than a prescription authority, and any chemical guidance should be confirmed against local agricultural-extension recommendations before field use.

\subsection{Training Methodology and Optimization}\label{subsec:training}

\textbf{Data Augmentation:} Standard image augmentation improves robustness to field variability. During training we apply random resized crops (scale 0.8--1.0) to $224 \times 224$, random horizontal flips, random rotations ($\pm15^{\circ}$), and light colour jitter (brightness, contrast and saturation, each $\pm10\%$). Validation and test images use a deterministic resize and centre crop. All inputs are normalised to ImageNet statistics for transfer learning.

\textbf{Loss Function:} The reported model is trained with standard categorical cross-entropy. The explanation and uncertainty channels operate post-hoc on the trained network---Grad-CAM++ on its last-stage activations and Monte Carlo Dropout at inference---and the model is well calibrated after a single temperature-scaling step (Section~\ref{subsec:explainability-analysis}), so no auxiliary explanation- or calibration-loss terms are required for the headline result. In the ablation (Section~\ref{subsec:comparative}) we additionally probe an explanation-consistency regulariser together with MC-Dropout to quantify their marginal effect.

\textbf{Optimization Protocol:} Training uses AdamW (weight decay 0.01) with a cosine-annealing learning-rate schedule from $10^{-4}$, batch size 64 (128 for the baseline and ablation runs), for 40 epochs, retaining the checkpoint with the best validation accuracy. Convergence took roughly 30 minutes on an NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada. All baselines (Table~\ref{tab:baselines}) and ablation configurations use the same optimiser, schedule, budget and augmentation, so that accuracy differences are attributable to the component under test.

\subsection{Experimental Design and Validation Strategy}\label{subsec:experimental}

The evaluation process addresses several goals such as classification accuracy, explanation quality and faithfulness, uncertainty calibration, and comparison with baseline procedures. The dataset was divided using stratified sampling to keep the class distribution constant. The division was approximately 70\% for training (7,300 images), 15\% for validation (1,567 images) and 15\% for testing (1,540 images), matching the per-class counts in Table~\ref{tab:split-counts}. The split is performed at the image level with a fixed random seed; near-duplicate images, identified by perceptual hashing (Hamming distance $\leq 5$), are kept within a single split to prevent leakage. This folded 1,669 near-duplicate images into their groups before assignment, and per-class proportions are preserved in every split. Stratification avoids bias in the evaluation sets for any disease group.
We evaluated the performance using standard classification measures, i.e. accuracy, precision, recall and F1-score calculated per-class and macro-averaged. We also calculated top-2 and top-3 accuracy, since in practical deployment settings, it is sometimes useful to give multiple diagnostic choices especially when co-occurrence of diseases is conceivable. Evaluating the quality of an explanation involves more advanced techniques than the usual accuracy measurements. We evaluate explanation quality with automatic, model-based metrics that require no human annotation. To test whether highlighted regions genuinely drive predictions, insertion and deletion scores were calculated \cite{petsiuk2018rise} by progressively revealing or removing the most-attributed regions and measuring the change in predicted probability. The continuous attribution maps were normalised to $[0,1]$, and the \emph{same} images and metrics were applied to every attribution method to keep the comparison fair. We additionally report per-method runtime. The uncertainty quantification study was based on calibration metrics: Expected Calibration Error (ECE), Maximum Calibration Error (MCE) and reliability diagrams. Well-calibrated models produce confidence scores indicative of empirical accuracy. Brier scores were also calculated to combine both discrimination (class separability) and calibration (confidence accuracy) into a single measure. 

The natural-language explanation layer is grounded in the curated knowledge base. A quantitative evaluation of the generated text---automatic text-similarity scores (BLEU/ROUGE) against reference descriptions, readability indices, and a human assessment with agricultural stakeholders---is planned as part of the future human-subjects study and is not reported in this paper.
We compared our model against the following baselines: a standard ConvNeXt V2-Tiny without explainability features, ConvNeXt V2-Tiny with Grad-CAM++ only, ConvNeXt V2-Tiny with uncertainty quantification only, alternative architectures (ResNet50, VGG16, Inception-v3, EfficientNet-B0), and traditional machine learning methods (SVM and Random Forest with handcrafted features) to quantify the improvement provided by deep learning. Component contributions were investigated using ablation studies. Monte Carlo Dropout was more computationally efficient for uncertainty quantification than deep ensemble approaches \cite{lakshminarayanan2017ensembles}, and ensembles achieved minor accuracy improvements that do not justify the practical deployment limits. The final design decisions were informed by systematic examination of data augmentation, loss function components, and natural language production strategy combinations.


\subsection{Computational Environment and Infrastructure}\label{subsec:computation}

Development and training were performed on a workstation with an NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada GPU (48GB VRAM) and CUDA 12.1 support, used to train ConvNeXt V2-Tiny at a batch size of 64. Data pretreatment and analysis duties were handled by the CPU (Intel Core i9-10900K with 32GB RAM). The software environment consisted of PyTorch 2.0 with torchvision 0.15 and the timm library for the ConvNeXt V2 backbone, the Hugging Face Transformers library (with bitsandbytes for 4-bit quantization) for the Phi-3-mini language model, the classic machine learning baseline scikit-learn 1.3, and the image processing library OpenCV 4.8. To keep track of the training process systematically, we used Weights \& Biases (wandb) for experiment tracking and hyperparameter optimization. All training and evaluation reported in this paper were run on the workstation described above; on-device edge (e.g.\ Jetson) and mobile benchmarking is left to future deployment work.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Classification Performance Analysis}\label{subsec:classification-performance}

The overall accuracy of the classification on the test set is 97.5\%, with macro-averaged precision 97.2\%, recall 96.6\% and F1-score 96.9\% over the 1{,}540 test images. The Paddy Doctor set has realistic field conditions---water droplets, varied angles, and inconsistent lighting---so this accuracy is a strong result for real-world deployment. The proposed model also improves on a ResNet50 baseline trained under identical settings (96.8\%), confirming the benefit of the modern ConvNeXt V2 backbone for agricultural disease identification.

The top-k accuracy is also relevant for decision support: top-1 97.5\%, top-2 99.5\%, and top-3 99.7\%, so when the top prediction is wrong the correct class is almost always within the top three. The per-class analysis is instructive. Dead Heart was classified best (precision 100\%, recall 99.5\%, F1 99.8\%), and Hispa (F1 98.4\%), Healthy (98.3\%), Bacterial Leaf Streak (98.0\%), Brown Spot (97.9\%) and Leaf Blast (97.6\%) were all above F1 97.5\%. Bacterial Leaf Blight reached perfect precision (100\%) but lower recall (90.5\%, F1 95.0\%), since some cases were predicted as Tungro. The hardest class was Downy Mildew (precision 90.7\%, recall 91.7\%, F1 91.2\%), whose diffuse symptoms overlap with several diseases.

The bacterial diseases are the subtlest to separate: bacterial leaf blight, leaf streak and panicle blight all present elongated marginal lesions with only fine distinguishing features (lesion width, margin texture) that challenge even trained diagnosticians. Tungro, whose orange-yellow discolouration is less localised than lesion diseases, was nonetheless classified well (precision 94.8\%, recall 96.7\%, F1 95.7\%).

The confusion matrix (Figure~\ref{fig:confusion-matrix}) shows a strong diagonal with few systematic errors. The largest off-diagonal confusion is Bacterial Leaf Blight predicted as Tungro (6.8\% of Bacterial Leaf Blight cases). Downy Mildew is the most spread-out class, leaking small amounts to Leaf Blast (3.1\%), Brown Spot (2.1\%) and Tungro (2.1\%). These mirror difficulties seen in plant pathology, where specialists also struggle to separate certain pairings without additional context. Most classes have negligible off-diagonal mass, consistent with the high per-class F1 scores.

\subsection{Explainability Analysis}\label{subsec:explainability-analysis}

We assess the faithfulness of Grad-CAM++ explanations with model-based metrics that require no human annotation, computed over a sample of test images. The mean insertion score---the rise in predicted probability as the most-attributed regions are progressively revealed---was 0.89, indicating that the regions Grad-CAM++ marks as important genuinely drive the model's prediction. Faithfulness is comparable across all six attribution methods (Table~\ref{tab:visual-explanation}); Grad-CAM++ attains it at interactive speed (0.05\,s per image) rather than the many seconds required by the perturbation-based methods (Section~\ref{subsec:comparative}).

A per-disease breakdown shows that explanation quality varies with symptom morphology. Lesion-based diseases (Leaf Blast, Brown Spot, Bacterial Leaf Blight), which present distinct localised symptoms, yield sharper and more faithful attributions than systemic diseases with diffuse symptoms (Tungro, Downy Mildew). This pattern reflects the natural explanatory advantage of diseases with distinct, readily apparent symptoms located in a specific area. Figure~\ref{fig:cam-examples} shows representative Grad-CAM++ visualisations on real paddy field images, where attention concentrates on the visible lesions in confident cases and the system defers to expert verification when the symptoms are atypical. Monte Carlo Dropout uncertainty quantification gives well-calibrated confidence estimates. The model is well calibrated: the Expected Calibration Error (ECE) is 0.015, falling to 0.007 after temperature scaling ($T=1.42$), with Maximum Calibration Error 0.42 and Brier score 0.040 (Figure~\ref{fig:reliability}).

The reliability diagram (Figure~\ref{fig:reliability}) shows close agreement between predicted confidence and empirical accuracy. Confidence discriminates errors well: predictions above 0.9 confidence (96\% of the test set) are 98.8\% accurate, whereas the few low-confidence predictions (below 0.7) are only 56\% accurate---exactly the cases the referral policy routes to an expert. Decomposing the MC-Dropout uncertainty gives a 9\%/91\% epistemic/aleatoric split: most residual uncertainty is aleatoric (intrinsic image ambiguity) rather than epistemic, indicating that the model is confident and that additional training data would yield limited further gains on this dataset.

The uncertainty estimates are greater for the morphologically complex diseases (Tungro, bacterial diseases) than for those with distinctive visual signatures (Leaf Blast, Brown Spot), so the user can route high-uncertainty cases to expert consultation. The natural-language explanation layer is grounded in the curated knowledge base (Section~\ref{subsec:explainability}); a quantitative human evaluation of its outputs is deferred to future work (see Limitations).

\subsection{Comparative Analysis with Baseline Methods}\label{subsec:comparative}

We compared ConvNeXt V2-Tiny against a set of baselines trained under identical splits, augmentation, optimiser and budget (Table~\ref{tab:baselines}): classic CNNs (ResNet50~\cite{he2016deep}, VGG16~\cite{simonyan2015vgg}, Inception-v3~\cite{szegedy2016inception}), an efficient CNN (EfficientNet-B0~\cite{tan2019efficientnet}), and recent lightweight vision models (MobileViT-S~\cite{mehta2022mobilevit}, EfficientFormer-L1~\cite{li2022efficientformer}). ConvNeXt V2-Tiny achieved the best accuracy (97.5\%) at a competitive parameter count (27.9M). All the modern backbones perform strongly on this dataset (95.0--96.8\%), but ConvNeXt V2-Tiny leads: ResNet50 reached 96.8\%, VGG16 96.6\% and EfficientFormer-L1 96.5\%, while EfficientNet-B0 (95.5\%), MobileViT-S (95.6\%) and Inception-v3 (95.0\%) trailed slightly. Beyond the small accuracy margin, the depthwise-convolution structure of ConvNeXt V2 produces hierarchical feature maps that support clean Grad-CAM++ localisation, which is why it is preferred for the explainability goals of this work.

We further isolated the contribution of the pipeline components through an ablation study (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}): starting from the backbone, we add the explanation-consistency loss and then MC-Dropout uncertainty, measuring the marginal effect on classification accuracy. The backbone alone reaches 97.3\%; adding the explanation-consistency loss gives a small gain to 97.7\% ($+0.4$), and inference-time MC-Dropout leaves accuracy essentially unchanged (97.6\%) while providing the calibrated uncertainty used by the referral policy. The uncertainty, language-explanation and recommendation components are not intended to raise classification accuracy; they add interpretability and decision support, and quantifying their benefit to end users is left to future human-subjects studies (see Limitations).

The attribution methods are contrasted in Table~\ref{tab:visual-explanation}, which reports model-based faithfulness and per-image runtime. All six methods achieve comparable faithfulness, but their cost differs sharply: the CAM-family methods (Grad-CAM, Grad-CAM++, LayerCAM) and Integrated Gradients run in well under a second per image (Grad-CAM++ at 0.05\,s), whereas LIME and SHAP require 14--16\,s, which rules them out for interactive field use. LIME also produced different explanations for similar inputs, reducing its reliability where consistency matters for adoption. Integrated Gradients is both faithful and fast (0.23\,s); we nonetheless select Grad-CAM++ because its region-level heatmaps align with how agronomists describe symptoms and are the fastest to compute, supporting interactive use on mobile and edge devices.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

This rigorous evaluation of explainable agricultural AI achieves 97.5\% accuracy on realistic field images while retaining full explainability, indicating that current designs can substantially reduce the classical performance--explainability trade-off. Under identical training, ConvNeXt V2-Tiny edges out strong modern baselines (ResNet50 96.8\%, VGG16 96.6\%, EfficientFormer-L1 96.5\%); the margin is small because all modern backbones perform well on this dataset, but ConvNeXt V2-Tiny additionally supports clean Grad-CAM++ localisation, which motivates its choice here.
The faithfulness measures (insertion 0.89; Table~\ref{tab:visual-explanation}) demonstrate that the visual explanations are genuinely loyal to the model's reasoning rather than ``after-the-fact'' rationalisations. The CAM-family methods achieve comparable faithfulness, and Grad-CAM++ does so at the lowest latency (0.05\,s), which is why it is preferred for interactive use. Attribution quality varied with symptom morphology, with distinct lesion-based diseases yielding sharper attributions than systemic diseases with diffuse symptoms. The uncertainty estimates are well calibrated (ECE 0.007 after temperature scaling) and provide reliable, actionable confidence for the referral policy.
In terms of deployment, the computational demand is modest. \emph{Measured} (NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada, batch size 1): the ConvNeXt V2-Tiny classifier runs in $\approx$10\,ms at 27.9M parameters ($\approx$112\,MB), and a Grad-CAM++ explanation adds only $\approx$0.05\,s, so prediction plus visual explanation is comfortably within interactive latency. The Monte Carlo Dropout uncertainty estimate (30 forward passes) and the offline Phi-3-mini language layer---quantized to 4-bit (NF4, GGUF, $\approx$2.1\,GB)---add further cost; on-device latency for these components, edge-device (e.g.\ Jetson) inference, and in-field battery use were \emph{not} benchmarked in this study and should be measured per target device before deployment. The offline LLM layer is the heaviest component ($\approx$2.1\,GB), so we recommend a hybrid deployment in which the classifier, visual heatmap and uncertainty estimate (the safety-critical channels) always run on-device, while the natural-language layer is offline-optional or cloud-assisted. The modular architecture also allows easy integration with existing agricultural information systems, IoT sensors and precision-agriculture platforms.

\textbf{Limitations:} Some limitations need to be considered. Firstly, the assessment is based on the Paddy Doctor dataset of specific geographical regions and may not be generalizable to other rice growing areas with various climatic circumstances, cultivars or disease symptoms. Secondly, this work evaluates the system with automatic, model-based metrics only; a human-subjects study quantifying how the explanations affect agricultural stakeholders' diagnostic accuracy and trust has not yet been conducted, and is essential future work before any claim of end-user benefit can be made. Thirdly, unlike template-based approaches, Phi-3-mini does not guarantee correctness in all cases of natural-language generation and may occasionally produce incorrect wording or statements, so prompt engineering and output checks must be applied carefully. Fourth, Monte Carlo Dropout can provide computationally cheap estimates of uncertainty, but it may miss some uncertainty types, such as uncertainty due to out-of-distribution inputs. Fifth, the 4-bit quantized LLM will require an additional 2.1GB of storage, which may be too much for some low-end devices. Finally, the focus on single-image diagnosis may not be adequate for scenarios where the farmers upload a sequence of photographs of the same plant or field to obtain a more thorough diagnosis.

\textbf{Future Directions:} There are several promising directions that should be pursued. There are methods for explaining more advanced, especially counterfactual and contrastive techniques which are specifically developed for agricultural contexts that could offer more intuitive explanations than current attention-based explanations. Combining multi-modal sensing (hyperspectral imaging and thermal cameras) may provide a huge improvement in diagnostics, particularly for diseases with subtle visual signs. Ongoing learning capabilities would enable the system to adjust to new variants of the disease and new environmental conditions without having to retrain the system completely. Federated learning models might help to improve the model collectively across different farms and regions, while maintaining data privacy, which could be essential for its implementation. Extension of other crop types is a logical next step, as is linking with precision agriculture technologies for automated early warning systems. Multilingual support will be crucial to disseminating to farmers across different linguistic areas and linkages to agricultural insurance schemes may offer extra motivation for uptake and offer useful data for risk assessment.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

In this work, we presented a comprehensive framework, ExplainableCropAI, that addresses the gap between performance and explainability. We integrated several explainable-AI techniques: Grad-CAM++ for visual explanations with improved localization, Monte Carlo Dropout for uncertainty quantification, and LLM-based natural language generation with Phi-3-mini for context-aware explanations. The system achieves 97.5\% classification accuracy with full explainability, leading strong modern baselines trained under identical settings (e.g.\ ResNet50 96.8\%). Visual explanations using Grad-CAM++ are faithful to the model's reasoning (insertion 0.89) and the fastest to compute (0.05\,s per image), and the uncertainty estimates are well calibrated (ECE 0.007 after temperature scaling), supporting an uncertainty-aware referral policy that lifts accuracy on retained cases from 97.5\% to 99.8\% at 30\% referral. Computational requirements remain practical for mobile deployment ($\approx$10\,ms inference and 27.9M parameters ($\approx$112\,MB) for the classifier, plus an optional 2.1\,GB for the quantized Phi-3-mini LLM).

The key contributions of this work are as follows. First, we demonstrate empirically that comprehensive explainability can be integrated into agricultural AI without prohibitive performance penalties. Second, we contribute a reproducible benchmark protocol for evaluating visual-explanation faithfulness and uncertainty calibration in crop-disease imaging. Third, we present a working technical framework that balances accuracy, explainability, and computational efficiency in deployable form. Fourth, our evaluation combines classification performance with automatic, model-based explanation-faithfulness and calibration metrics; a human-subjects evaluation of end-user benefit remains important future work.

The full implementation---model, Grad-CAM++ explainer, Monte Carlo Dropout uncertainty estimation, the LLM natural-language generator with its curated agronomic knowledge base, and the reproducible experiment scripts for data splitting, explanation-method comparison, calibration, and baseline/ablation studies---is open-sourced at \url{https://github.com/santoshray02/ExplainableCropAI} and archived with a permanent DOI at Zenodo (\url{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21030215}). Trained model weights and loading instructions are provided in the repository README, together with an \texttt{environment.yml} for environment reproduction.
