%% Abstract

\abstract{Deep learning models have demonstrated strong feasibility for crop disease diagnosis in terms of precision; however, their black-box nature limits practical usage. Farmers and agricultural professionals need to understand AI diagnosis before they can be used in decision making. In this study, we propose ExplainableCropAI, a system that unites technical performance and real-world applicability. The framework integrates three complementary explainability techniques: Grad-CAM++, a visual heatmap for enhancing localization performance; Monte Carlo Dropout, an uncertainty quantification technique; and LLM-based natural language generation with Phi-3-mini, which produces context-aware, audience-adaptive explanations. The design treats crop diagnosis as a human-centred visual decision-support problem, coupling visual evidence localisation with an uncertainty-aware visual interface and language-based explanation. It was fully explainable and achieved 91.3\% accuracy on a Paddy Doctor dataset of 10,407 photos spanning 10 classes (nine diseases and healthy), using a ConvNeXt V2-Tiny model, an up-to-date CNN design with depthwise convolutions, LayerNorm and GELU activations. The system couples calibrated uncertainty estimates with faithful visual attributions and grounded language explanations, evaluated with automatic, reproducible metrics. This highlights the potential to balance the accuracy of categorization with the interpretability of the model in agricultural AI systems.}

\keywords{Visual decision support, Human-centred visual computing, Visual evidence localisation, ConvNeXt V2-Tiny model, Explainable artificial intelligence, Grad-CAM++, Paddy disease detection, Uncertainty quantification, Visual explanations}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

For 3.5 billion people in Asia, Africa and Europe, rice is the most popular food among all staple crops. Rice production is practiced on 167 million hectares globally and 144 million agricultural households rely on rice farming \cite{kamilaris2018deep}. Annual losses from crop failure due to diseases are estimated to be 125-180 million tonnes and this greatly affects the economics of agriculture \cite{savary2019global,rahman2020deep}. More than 70 diseases are known to affect rice crops and many of them show identical symptoms during the early crop appearance stage. Manual diagnosis of disease can be done with mere 23-47\% accuracy depending on the individual’s evaluation and environmental conditions \cite{bock2021severity,nutter1993assessing}. The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) states that pests and illnesses cause an annual loss of 20--40\% of the world's crop production, costing around US\$220 billion \cite{fao2020planthealth,fao2021pests} every year. Visual disease assessment studies have reported intraclass correlation coefficients between 0.60 and 0.85 \cite{mrisho2020accuracy,oerke2020sensor}. Delays from initial observation to sampling to expert consultation along the diagnostic pipeline extend the temporal frame beyond which diseases can be treated effectively, e.g., quickly progressing diseases \cite{ristaino2021persistent}. This is a difficulty that calls for automated and scalable diagnostics.

Deep learning has changed the way agricultural illnesses are diagnosed. CNNs have been found to be accurate at > 95\% on datasets of plant disease \cite{mohanty2016using}, but typically on selected datasets in controlled circumstances. Common image processing methods in the early 2000s had an accuracy of 60--75\%, in the 2010s machine learning techniques with handcrafted features could achieve an accuracy of 80--85\% and current deep learning architectures are able to exceed 95\% accuracy on benchmark datasets \cite{liakos2018machine,shafay2025review,akter2024riceleafcnn}. However, when deploying the model to real farm circumstances, a reported degradation of accuracy of 15-22\% is found when trained on selected data sets \cite{mrisho2020accuracy}. ResNet architectures are able to achieve 92--96\% performance on different Crops with the good computational efficiency of the ResNet networks \cite{he2016deep,ferentinos2018deep}.  YOLO-based architectures have also been applied to real-time field detection \cite{fuentes2017robust}, including our prior YOLOv8-based detection framework on the same Paddy Doctor dataset \cite{kumar2026yolo}. ConvNeXt V2 was launched in \cite{woo2023convnext,wang2024wheatconvnext} in 2023, which merges the efficiency of CNN with the design principles of the transformer, achieving state-of-the-art results by employing depthwise convolutions, LayerNorm, and GELU activation function. 
Rice cultivation has some different type of imaging challenges like the fields are flooded, humidity is high, the canopy is dense with complicated light and occlusion circumstances. Using CNN customization and paddy specific augmentation procedures, Rahman et al. \cite{rahman2020deep} achieved an accuracy of 94.33\%. Transfer learning may be used to reduce the training time and training data, which is done by leveraging the ImageNet data \cite{lu2017identification}.



Although technology is quickly advancing, AI has not been widely adopted in agriculture, partly due to trust difficulties \cite{wolfert2017big,ahmad2024nextgen,gardezi2024aitrust}. Farm decisions have a huge financial impact. A false diagnosis means inappropriate pesticides being used, and the harm to crops leads to losses in income. Acceptance by farmers needs to be conveyed. To solve these problems, there are numerous XAI methods: For the predictions \cite{selvaraju2017grad}, Grad-CAM generates heat maps for the pertinent sections of the image. Grad-CAM++ \cite{chattopadhay2018grad,khan2024potato} developed second-order gradient weighting to improve localization accuracy, and Grad-CAM-based attribution has been deployed on edge devices for real-time crop disease diagnosis \cite{karim2024grapegradcam}. SHAP provides explanations in an intuitive game-theoretic format that supports pixel-level explanations but has high computing costs \cite{lundberg2017unified}; recent work unifies class-activation mapping with Shapley-value attribution for more faithful, theoretically grounded localization \cite{cai2025shapleycam}. LIME, by contrast, provides explanations by perturbing the input \cite{ribeiro2016should} and has low computational costs.

But visual explanations are not enough, confidence estimate is also needed. Uncertainty quantification is performed with several inference passes with active dropout \cite{gal2016dropout}, which may be decomposed into aleatoric (data noise) and epistemic (model knowledge) components \cite{kendall2017uncertainties} via Monte Carlo Dropout. This information of uncertainty is useful in making proper treatment selections. The other is natural language explanations. Template-based solutions produce organized output but lack the flexibility required for complex diagnostic circumstances. They can provide context-aware and audience-adaptive explanations, such as explaining the complete range of disease manifestations, as proven by Large Language Models (LLMs) \cite{abdin2024phi3,zhou2025llmcrop,singh2024llmagri}. Visual-language foundation models have recently been applied to disease diagnosis and human--AI co-decision, underscoring the value of language-based interfaces for expert--user collaboration \cite{yao2025vlmdiagnosis}. Farmers adopt research advances when they trust them, they are culturally acceptable, and they are economically viable \cite{lee2004trust}. Explainability helps to mitigate the trust gap by enabling informed decision making.

In this study, an explainable paddy disease classification system is developed to overcome the trust gap between the applicability of AI and farmers' adoption. Whereas our prior work \cite{kumar2026yolo} optimised YOLOv8 for high-accuracy paddy disease \emph{detection}, the present work shifts the focus from raw detection accuracy to \emph{interpretable, uncertainty-aware} classification and decision support for agricultural stakeholders. The three main challenges are: (i) embedding multi-level explainability mechanisms into state-of-the-art CNN architectures, (ii) quantifying explanation faithfulness and uncertainty calibration with automatic, reproducible metrics, and (iii) optimising the system for edge deployment without compromising accuracy or explainability.

This work approaches crop disease diagnosis as a visual decision-support problem, emphasising visual evidence localisation, uncertainty-aware visual interfaces, and the human-centred interpretation of image-based models. This work makes the following contributions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Visual Evidence Localisation Benchmark}: Under identical settings, we compare six attribution methods (Grad-CAM, Grad-CAM++, LayerCAM, Integrated Gradients, LIME, and SHAP) using model-based faithfulness metrics (insertion/deletion), attribution stability, and runtime, yielding a reusable benchmark for visual-explanation quality in crop-disease imaging.

    \item \textbf{Multi-level Explanation System}: A novel framework that combines Grad-CAM++ for visual heatmaps with improved localization, Monte Carlo Dropout for uncertainty quantification (30 stochastic passes), and Phi-3-mini LLM (3.8B parameters) for context-aware natural language generation.

    \item \textbf{ConvNeXt V2-Tiny Integration}: We apply the ConvNeXt V2-Tiny architecture to paddy disease diagnosis with full support for gradient-based explanation methods (10,407 images, 10 disease classes, and 91.3\% accuracy).

    \item \textbf{Deployment-Optimized Quantization}: For resource-limited farmers, 4-bit quantization for LLM size reduction from 7.6GB to 2.1GB (73\%) with deployment optimization strategies specific to agriculture to maintain explanation quality to enable deployment on smart phones.

    \item \textbf{Audience-Adaptive Generation}: LLM-based explanation system that tailors output complexity to user expertise level, providing actionable recommendations for farmers and technical depth for extension officers.
\end{itemize}

\section{Materials and Methods}\label{sec:methods}

The system comprises five interconnected components (Figure~\ref{fig:system-architecture}): 1. the ConvNeXt V2-Tiny model for disease classification, 2. the Grad-CAM++ module for generating visual explanations, 3. Monte Carlo Dropout for quantifying uncertainty, 4. a natural language generator (LLM) for textual explanations (Phi-3-mini), and 5. a recommendation engine for treatment suggestions. The modular structure allows for optimizing each module separately, while preserving system coherence.

One of the design considerations was deployability flexibility. Multiple operation modes: complete cloud computing, edge computing, hybrid (distributed computation). Also the system provides various levels of explanation based on the competence of the user. Recommendations are straightforward and practical for the farmers and technical assessments are offered in detail for the extension staff. The visual heatmaps are accompanied by uncertainty scores and written explanations, thus constituting a holistic decision support system as depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:explanation-flowchart}. The integrated decision-support interface that presents these components to the user is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:user-interface}.

\subsection{Dataset and Preprocessing}\label{subsec:dataset}

In this work, we use the Paddy Doctor dataset \cite{petchiammal2023paddy} which contains 10,407 high resolution photos ($2448 \times 2448$ pixels) of nine disease categories and healthy plants. Images were captured under real field circumstances with natural light at different development phases to generate a good range of variability for realistic deployment scenarios. The distribution of the classes is shown in Table~\ref{tab:dataset-distribution}. The most frequent class is Healthy (16.9\%) and the least frequent is Bacterial Panicle Blight (3.2\%), a roughly 5:1 imbalance that we control through stratified splitting and inverse-frequency class weighting. This pattern is comparable to the natural illness incidence in rice cultivation. The exact per-class counts in each split are reported in Table~\ref{tab:split-counts}.

We use the dataset's provided expert labels as released, without additional re-annotation. The images were pre-processed by resizing to $640 \times 640$ to balance the cost of computation with the level of detail required for enhancement of subtle disease presentations, Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) to improve local contrast, color space transformations to optimally highlight disease-specific features, and normalization to ImageNet statistics for transfer learning.

\subsection{Deep Learning Architecture and Modifications}\label{subsec:dl-architecture}

We select ConvNeXt V2-Tiny as the backbone model, which is a modern design of CNN that incorporates several significant advances of transformer models while preserving the efficiency of CNN \cite{woo2023convnext}. The ConveXt V2 released in 2023 adopts depthwise separable convolutions that significantly reduce the computational costs, without reducing the representation power. The architecture also switches to using LayerNorm instead of BatchNorm for training stability and uses GELU (Gaussian Error Linear Unit) activations for smoother gradients than ReLU. ConvNeXt V2-Tiny has 28.6 million parameters and offers a compelling trade-off between model size and efficiency, making it ideal for mobile use. Most crucially, ConvNeXt V2 has a rigorously ordered hierarchical feature extraction structure, providing strong support for gradient-based feature-explanation methods such as Grad-CAM++. The attention mechanism of Vision Transformers produces explanations that are less obvious for agricultural stakeholders compared to saliency maps created by a CNN, despite comparable accuracy when evaluated on benchmark datasets. The depthwise convolution structure of ConvNeXt V2 creates localised feature responses that readily map to interpretable visual explanations, making it very suitable for our explainability aims.

We modified the standard ConvNeXt V2-Tiny architecture in various ways: The last classification head was replaced to predict 10 classifications (9 diseases and healthy). To generate high-quality Grad-CAM++ heat maps, the global average pooling layer was retained before classification to preserve spatial information. To conduct multi-scale analysis, intermediate feature extraction points are added at each stage and statistics for LayerNorm are tuned for Agricultural images, which have distinct statistics from ImageNet dataset used for pre-training. The dropout arrangement serves two purposes. Separate dropout rates (0.2, 0.3, and 0.5) are used in different blocks during training to minimize overfitting. Active dropout can be utilized to carry out many forward passes to estimate uncertainty from Monte Carlo Dropout without introducing architectural complexity, and also provide uncertainty estimates during the inference phase.

Training was place in three phases. For learning rate 0.001, phase one (epochs 1-20) was used to train only the classification head, freezing the backbone of the network (the part pre-trained on ImageNet). This allowed the network to adapt the classification head task without altering the pre-trained network features. In phase two (epochs 21-60) layers were progressively unfrozen with the discriminative learning rates (1:3:10 ratio), where the earliest layers learnt slower to maintain in contact with the broad characteristics, the later layers more aggressively with the task specific patterns. Phase three (epochs 61--100) was the full fine-tuning (FT) on ag-specific augmentations at lower learning rate (LR). This stage-wise training strategy substantially outperformed end-to-end training from startup.

\subsection{Explainability Mechanism}\label{subsec:explainability}

\textbf{Grad-CAM++ Visual Explanations:} We use Grad-CAM++ \cite{chattopadhay2018grad} which is an improved version of Grad-CAM, which uses second-order gradients to achieve better localization accuracy. In contrast to the standard Grad-CAM, which relies on the first-order gradients to assign weights to contributions from feature maps, Grad-CAM++ calculates pixel-wise weights using both first-order and second-order partial gradients, which allows it to perform better on multiple occurrences of the same object class and provide more accurate localization of disease symptoms in complex agricultural images.

In the Grad-CAM++ process, feature maps are extracted from the last convolutional layer of ConvNeXt V2-Tiny, and second-order gradient information is calculated and used to determine the weights for the weighted combination of feature maps. Such method is able to generate more fine-grained attention maps that give more accurate highlight to disease-affected regions, especially useful when dealing with diseases that have multiple lesions or diffuse symptom patterns. ReLU activation throws away negative contributions and concentrates on features that aid the prediction. It produces a heatmap which is upsampled to a size appropriate to the input image, which is then overlaid for visualization, with the choice of jet or viridis colormaps being determined by user preference testing. Instead, it also tested LayerCAM \cite{jiang2021layercam} to produce explanations from a variety of network layers and fuse these layers for more extensive spatial coverage. LayerCAM proved to be beneficial with early stages of diseases with minor symptoms, meanwhile Grad-CAM++ was more effective with well developed disease symptoms and was chosen as the main explanation method.

\textbf{Monte Carlo Dropout for Uncertainty Quantification:} We use dropout during training and take 30 forward passes with different dropout patterns instead of the typical inference with dropout disabled. Model uncertainty is measured by prediction variance across runs where high agreement means high confidence and high variance means significant uncertainty; this approach has proven effective for uncertainty-aware plant disease diagnosis \cite{pugazhendi2026sugarcanemcdropout}. The uncertainty can thus be decomposed into data (intrinsic uncertainty) and model (extrinsic uncertainty). Epistemic uncertainty is particularly useful, because it tells us that the training data could be valuable. The temperature scaling approach \cite{guo2017calibration} tries to align the empirical accuracy with the raw uncertainty estimations.

\textbf{LLM-Based Natural Language Generation:} Instead of using templates, we use the compact Large Language Model (LLM) Phi-3-mini \cite{abdin2024phi3} to produce context-aware and audience-adaptive explanations. This LLM-based method allows more flexible and natural language generation, which can account for the various symptom clusters and situational nuances of disease that a fixed template can't handle.

Phi-3-mini takes structured input comprising the anticipated disease class, the confidence scores, the uncertainty estimates, the discovered visual features from the Grad-CAM++ analysis, and the target audience specification (farmer vs. extension officer). The model produces explanations depending on the diagnostic scenario and user knowledge level. Farmer-oriented explanations emphasize clarity and actionability (e.g., ``This looks like Leaf Blast with 91\% confidence. Notice the gray oval dots with brown margins on the leaves – this is typical of this fungal illness. 'Consider a fungicide spray in the following 2-3 days. ''). Extension officer explanations add technical depth, including differential diagnostic considerations (e.g. “Primary diagnosis: Leaf Blast (91\% confidence). Brown Spot (5\%) and early Bacterial Leaf Blight (3\%) as differential. Lesion morphologoy shows distinctive diamond shaped progression. Microscopic confirmation is necessary if there is an uncommon edge necrosis pattern. ''). For mobile deployment, we employ 4-bit quantization (GGUF format) which reduces the model size from around 7.6GB to 2.1GB while preserving the quality of explanations. This quantized model provides 1.2-2.0 seconds inference time on contemporary smartphones enabling realistic field deployment without the need for cloud connectivity.

\textbf{Safety and grounding of recommendations.} Because inappropriate pesticide or treatment advice carries economic and environmental risk, the language model \emph{rephrases} rather than \emph{originates} agronomic content. All treatment, prevention, severity and chemical-class statements are supplied to the model as typed fields drawn from a curated knowledge base compiled from public agricultural-extension references (e.g., the IRRI Rice Knowledge Bank and state agricultural-university extension material); the model is constrained to these fields and does not generate free-form dosages. A template-based generator reproduces the same knowledge-base content verbatim whenever the LLM is unavailable or fails, so the system never relies on unconstrained generation for actionable advice. The system also applies confidence- and agreement-based thresholds: high confidence with high inter-pass agreement yields a direct diagnosis; moderate values add explicit verification-advised wording; and low confidence or high uncertainty triggers a ``consult an agricultural expert'' referral with no firm treatment asserted. ExplainableCropAI is therefore positioned as decision \emph{support} rather than a prescription authority, and any chemical guidance should be confirmed against local agricultural-extension recommendations before field use.

\subsection{Training Methodology and Optimization}\label{subsec:training}

\textbf{Data Augmentation:} A lot of data augmentation helped the model to be robust to actual world fluctuation. Geometric transforms featured random rotations ($\pm15$ degrees), horizontal and vertical flips (50\% chance), random crops at scales 0.8--1.0, and elastic and perspective warping. Photometric augmentations included brightness change ($\pm20\%$), contrast change ($\pm25\%$), hue change ($\pm10$ degrees), saturation change ($\pm15\%$) and Gaussian noise ($\sigma = 0.01$). Domain-specific augmentations proved very useful: generated water droplets on leaves (common in paddy field conditions), synthetic shadows, wind impacts on the blur, and depth-of-field adjustments. Mixup augmentation \cite{zhang2018mixup} was tested but did not increase performance on this classification assignment.

\textbf{Loss Function:} The loss function extends beyond standard classification to incorporate explainability constraints:
\begin{equation}
L_{total} = \lambda_{ce} \times L_{ce} + \lambda_{att} \times L_{attention} + \lambda_{cal} \times L_{calibration} + \lambda_{div} \times L_{diversity}
\end{equation}

The main component is categorical cross-entropy ($\lambda_{ce} = 1.0$) with inverse frequency weighting ($w_i = N/(C \times n_i)$) for class balancing. We added three new terms: the attention consistency loss ($\lambda_{att}=0.1$) to make sure Grad-CAM explanations are consistent for similar inputs, the calibration loss ($\lambda_{cal}=0.05$) to guarantee accurate uncertainty estimation, and the diversity loss ($\lambda_{div}=0.02$) to prevent too much reliance on a few features.

\textbf{Optimization Protocol:} Training was performed with AdamW with weight decay of .01 and cosine annealing learning rate schedule from .001 to .0001. The batch size was set to 16 because of the limit of the GPU memory and the training stability. Gradient clipping (max norm 1.0) stopped gradient explosion. Training was terminated early if the validation loss did not improve for 15 consecutive epochs. Convergence was usually observed between epochs 80--100 and took about 6--8 hours on an RTX 3090. Hyperparameter optimization was performed using Optuna for a Bayesian search over learning rates, loss weights, dropout rates and augmentation intensities.

\subsection{Experimental Design and Validation Strategy}\label{subsec:experimental}

The evaluation process addresses several goals such as classification accuracy, explanation quality and faithfulness, uncertainty calibration, and comparison with baseline procedures. The dataset was divided using stratified sampling to keep the class distribution constant. The division was 70\% for training (7,286 images), 15\% for validation (1,562 images) and 15\% for testing (1,559 images), matching the per-class counts in Table~\ref{tab:split-counts}. The split is performed at the image level with a fixed random seed; near-duplicate images, identified by perceptual hashing (Hamming distance $\leq 5$), are kept within a single split to prevent leakage, and per-class proportions are preserved in every split. Stratification was used to avoid possible bias in the evaluation sets for certain disease groups. In addition, 5-fold cross validation is performed, stratified sample in each step. This approach generated five independent experiments with confidence ranges for all indicators and the opportunity to test the statistical significance of performance differences.
We evaluated the performance using standard classification measures, i.e. accuracy, precision, recall and F1-score calculated per-class and macro-averaged. We also calculated top-2 and top-3 accuracy, since in practical deployment settings, it is sometimes useful to give multiple diagnostic choices especially when co-occurrence of diseases is conceivable. Evaluating the quality of an explanation involves more advanced techniques than the usual accuracy measurements. We evaluate explanation quality with automatic, model-based metrics that require no human annotation. To test whether highlighted regions genuinely drive predictions, insertion and deletion scores were calculated \cite{petsiuk2018rise} by progressively revealing or removing the most-attributed regions and measuring the change in predicted probability. Continuous Grad-CAM++ maps were normalised to $[0,1]$ and binarised at a fixed threshold, and the \emph{same} threshold and metrics were applied to every attribution method to keep the comparison fair. We additionally report attribution stability (the variation of explanations under small input perturbations) and per-method runtime. The uncertainty quantification study was based on calibration metrics: Expected Calibration Error (ECE), Maximum Calibration Error (MCE) and reliability diagrams. Well-calibrated models produce ratings that are indicative of empirical accuracy rates. Brier scores were also calculated to combine both discrimination (class separability) and calibration (confidence accuracy) into a single measure. 

The quality of the natural language explanation was evaluated by computing the BLEU and ROUGE scores comparing the generated text with the curated knowledge-base reference descriptions for each disease, measuring how faithfully the model reproduces the grounded reference content. Readability was assessed using Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level and Automated Readability Index (ARI) to ensure suitable level of linguistic complexity for the target audience. 
We compared our model against the following baselines: a standard ConvNeXt V2-Tiny without explainability features, ConvNeXt V2-Tiny with Grad-CAM++ only, ConvNeXt V2-Tiny with uncertainty quantification only, alternative architectures (ResNet50, VGG16, Inception-v3, EfficientNet-B0), and traditional machine learning methods (SVM and Random Forest with handcrafted features) to quantify the improvement provided by deep learning. Component contributions were investigated using ablation studies. Monte Carlo Dropout was more computationally efficient for uncertainty quantification than deep ensemble approaches \cite{lakshminarayanan2017ensembles}, and ensembles achieved minor accuracy improvements that do not justify the practical deployment limits. The final design decisions were informed by systematic examination of data augmentation, loss function components, and natural language production strategy combinations.


\subsection{Computational Environment and Infrastructure}\label{subsec:computation}

Development and training were performed on a deep learning workstation with an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU (24GB VRAM) and CUDA 11.8 support, used to train ConvNeXt V2-Tiny at a batch size of 16. Data pretreatment and analysis duties were handled by the CPU (Intel Core i9-10900K with 32GB RAM). The software environment consisted of PyTorch 2.0 with torchvision 0.15 and the timm library for the ConvNeXt V2 backbone, the Hugging Face Transformers library (with bitsandbytes for 4-bit quantization) for the Phi-3-mini language model, the classic machine learning baseline scikit-learn 1.3, and the image processing library OpenCV 4.8. To keep track of the training process systematically, we used Weights \& Biases (wandb) for experiment tracking and hyperparameter optimization. For deployment testing, we tested on different hardware setups to test realistic deployment scenarios. We tested on an NVIDIA Jetson Xavier NX for edge computing assessment, different cellphones (Android and iOS) for mobile deployment testing, and standard laptops. System behavior was thoroughly confirmed in different computing settings through testing on several platforms.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Classification Performance Analysis}\label{subsec:classification-performance}

The overall accuracy of the classification on the test set is 91.3\%, the precision is 90.7\%, the recall is 90.2\% and the F1-score is 90.4\%. Therefore the following results can be interpreted: Claims of accuracies above 95\% are usually based on a well-controlled laboratory dataset and ideal imaging settings. The Paddy Doctor set has realistic field settings with water droplets, various angles, and inconsistent light with accuracy of 91.3\%, which is a very good result for implementation in real applications. The suggested CNN results in better accuracy when compared with the previous technique based on ResNet50 (85.2\%) proving the success of the proposed CNN in the identification of agricultural disease.

Five fold cross validation with a mean accuracy of 90.8\% with standard deviation of $\pm 1.1\%$ confirmed the stability of the model. The small standard deviation indicates that the model is not learning the characteristics of this data set but rather is generalizing. An examination of top-k accuracy reveals that clinical decision support can be used. The top-1 accuracy was 91.3\%, top-2 accuracy was 96.8\%, and top-3 accuracy was 98.4\%. This means that if the first guess is inaccurate, the correct answer is often among the top three supplied, allowing for the presentation of numerous solutions for the expert to adjudicate. There is instructive variety in analysis for each class. The best classification result was obtained for the healthy class (precision 99.3\%, recall 98.9\%, F1 99.1\%); this is particularly essential since false positives will result in wasteful pesticide consumption, with economic and environmental implications. Leaf Blast obtained the highest disease classification ratings (precision: 92.1\%, recall: 90.8\% and F1: 91.4\%) due to the characteristic circular form of the lesions with gray centers and brown edges. Brown Spot also performed well with the same concentric ring patterns as its visual signatures for all phases of the disease (precision: 89.3\%, recall: 87.6\%, F1: 88.4\%). Bacterial leaf blight was more difficult because of the larger morphological variety of elongated lesions with wavy margins (precision: 86.7\%, recall: 84.2\%, F1: 85.4\%).

The largest amount of uncertainty was between bacterial leaf blight and bacterial leaf streak (12.3\%). Both bacterial leaf blight and bacterial leaf streak showed elongated lesions along the margins of the leaves with subtle distinguishing features such as lesion width and margin characteristics that are difficult to separate even for trained diagnosticians. Since the tungro virus can express a wide spectrum of symptoms depending on plant variety, age, virus strain and environmental factors, its detection had moderate performance (Precision: 81.4\%, Recall: 79.8\%, F1: 80.6\%). Patterns of orange yellow discoloration are not as clear as lesion diseases.

By examining the detailed confusion matrix, there are some patterns of systematic errors that give clues as to the behaviors of the model and how to improve it. The confusion matrix (see Figure~\ref{fig:confusion-matrix}) shows that the overall accuracy for the classification is good; this can be seen from the high number of cells in the diagonal, and a few misclassifications can be seen in the off-diagonal parts. The maximum confusion is seen in morphologically comparable pairings of illnesses such as bacterial leaf blight with bacterial leaf streak (8.7\% confusion, bidirectional), brown spot with leaf blast (6.4\% confusion) and early-stage Hispa with Tungro (5.2\% confusion). Certain patterns of confusion are similar to what is seen in plant pathology, where disease specialists also struggle to tell certain pairings of diseases apart without supplementary contextual information. In numerous cases the patterns of perplexity are asymmetric, showing systemic bias in the decision making of the model. For instance, Bacterial Leaf Streak was misclassified as Bacterial Leaf Blight more often (11.2\%) than Bacterial Leaf Blight was misclassified as Bacterial Leaf Streak (6.3\%). This may be because Bacterial Leaf Blight occurs more often in the training data, so the model captures its characteristic features more strongly.

The severity-based research shows that the classification accuracy is lower for the early diseases (78.4\%) compared to the advanced diseases (91.7\%). The model has been successful in predicting severe disease symptoms, but has not demonstrated strong success in early identification—this is critical for successful therapy, and warrants additional studies.

\subsection{Explainability Analysis}\label{subsec:explainability-analysis}

We assess the faithfulness of Grad-CAM++ explanations with model-based metrics that require no human annotation. The examination of insertion scores (change in performance as the highlighted region is progressively revealed) yielded a mean score of 0.72, indicating that the regions indicated as important by Grad-CAM++ do contribute to the model’s prediction. Moreover, as the deletion score (0.21) indicates, the explanations are truthful because the removal of highlighted sections has a significant impact on model performance.

A per-disease breakdown shows that explanation quality varies with symptom morphology. Lesion-based diseases (Leaf Blast, Brown Spot, Bacterial Leaf Blight), which present distinct localised symptoms, yield sharper and more faithful attributions than systemic diseases with diffuse symptoms (Tungro, Downy Mildew). This pattern reflects the natural explanatory advantage of diseases with distinct, readily apparent symptoms located in a specific area. Figure~\ref{fig:cam-examples} shows representative Grad-CAM++ visualisations on real paddy field images, where attention concentrates on the visible lesions in confident cases and the system defers to expert verification when the symptoms are atypical. Monte Carlo Dropout uncertainty quantification gives well-calibrated confidence estimates that correspond appropriately to the accuracy of the prediction. The Expected Calibration Error (ECE) study showed relatively acceptable calibration. The ECE values suggest a reasonable relationship between confidence scores and accuracy rates.

Results from the reliability diagram analysis demonstrates that there is high agreement on the relation between predicted confidence and actual accuracy set for a range of confidence levels. There is good discrimination between high- and low-confidence predictions: 94.2\% accuracy for high-confidence predictions (c > 0.9) versus 67.8\% for low-confidence predictions (c < 0.5), which is useful for practical decision-making. We find epistemic uncertainty (model uncertainty) is responsible for 60\% of the predictive uncertainty while aleatoric uncertainty (inherent data noise) is responsible for 40\% of predictive uncertainty, using the method of uncertainty decomposition. The decomposition implies that more training data might significantly reduce the uncertainty, offering a way of defining priorities for data collection in the future, as well as for defining the model's scope. 

The uncertainty estimates are greater for morphologically complex diseases (Tungro, bacterial diseases) than for diseases with distinctive visual signatures (Leaf Blast, Brown Spot). The pattern suggests that the uncertainty estimates reflect the difficulty of diagnosis, so that the user can route high-uncertainty cases to expert consultation. The natural language explanations were assessed with automated text-quality measures. The BLEU score comparing the generated text against the curated knowledge-base reference descriptions was 0.73, indicating close semantic alignment with the grounded reference content.

Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level analysis ensured optimal readability: for farmer-oriented explanations Flesch-Kincaid Grade level was 8.2, while the extension officer explanations' score was 11.4, which matched the reader's scores. These scores allow explanations to be accessible for all users but give the required technical detail to various user groups.

\subsection{Comparative Analysis with Baseline Methods}\label{subsec:comparative}

We compared ConvNeXt V2-Tiny against a broad set of baselines under identical splits, augmentation, and training budget (Table~\ref{tab:baselines}): classic CNNs (ResNet50~\cite{he2016deep}, VGG16~\cite{simonyan2015vgg}, Inception-v3~\cite{szegedy2016inception}), an efficient CNN (EfficientNet-B0~\cite{tan2019efficientnet}), recent lightweight vision models (MobileViT-S~\cite{mehta2022mobilevit}, EfficientFormer-L1~\cite{li2022efficientformer}), a fine-grained recognition baseline (API-Net~\cite{zhuang2020apinet}), and traditional machine-learning methods with handcrafted features. ConvNeXt V2-Tiny achieved the best accuracy (91.3\%) at a competitive parameter count (28.6M) and inference time (28ms). ResNet50 (85.2\%) and Inception-v3 (86.1\%) were markedly lower, and Inception-v3 incurred a longer inference time (47ms) that limits mobile viability. EfficientNet-B0 (83.7\%) and the lightweight transformers (MobileViT-S 84.6\%, EfficientFormer-L1 86.4\%) traded accuracy for size and latency. The fine-grained baseline improved over plain CNNs (87.2\%) but did not match the modern backbone, while incurring higher latency. Crucially, the depthwise-convolution structure of ConvNeXt V2 yields cleaner Grad-CAM++ localisation than EfficientNet-B0 and Inception-v3, whose architectures degrade attribution quality. Traditional machine-learning baselines with handcrafted features were far weaker (SVM-HOG 71.2\%, Random Forest 68.7\%, k-NN-SIFT 64.3\%), confirming that deep representations are essential for this task.

We further isolated the contribution of each design choice through an ablation study (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). Starting from the plain backbone (89.4\%), the multi-scale feature pyramid~\cite{lin2017fpn} (+0.7\%), attention-consistency loss (+0.5\%), diversity loss (+0.2\%), calibration loss (+0.2\%, which also lowered ECE from 0.142 to 0.087), and class-balanced weighting (+0.3\%) each contributed measurable benefit, reaching the full 91.3\%. The uncertainty, language-explanation, and recommendation components do not change classification accuracy; they target the interpretability and decision-support qualities of the system rather than its predictive performance, and quantifying their benefit to end users is left to future human-subjects studies (see Limitations). As indicated previously, the attribution methods are carefully contrasted in Table \ref{tab:visual-explanation}, highlighting the trade-offs between faithfulness, stability, and computational cost. LIME generated different explanations for comparable inputs, making its conclusions less dependable for some applications such as agriculture where consistent results are required for user approval. On the other hand, SHAP explanations had a higher computational overhead, taking 3.2s for an explanation, compared to 0.4s for Grad-CAM, with comparable faithfulness. The per-pixel attributions that SHAP produces are a detailed explanation, but this can be too much information for someone in an agricultural context who wants a simplified explanation to take some action.
Vanilla CAM~\cite{zhou2016cam} was lower because of architectural limitations and obtained inferior faithfulness (lowest insertion and highest deletion in Table~\ref{tab:visual-explanation}). CAM provides coarser explanations that are not sufficient to show the exact location of the disease in complicated agricultural imagery. Integrated Gradients explanations provide high-quality, faithful attributions but are very computationally expensive (2.8s per explanation), therefore preventing real-time use. It has been described as technically superior in some areas, but computational constraints make it infeasible for resource-poor agricultural environments.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

This rigorous evaluation of explainable agricultural AI achieves 91.3\% accuracy. This is close to the $>95\%$ reported for black-box models on well-controlled datasets, yet retains full explainability, indicating that current designs can substantially reduce the classical performance--explainability trade-off. The employed ConvNeXt V2-Tiny with depthwise convolutions and new normalisation techniques resulted in an improvement of roughly 6\% in the prediction accuracy compared to the previous ResNet50-based methods. Cross validation stability (CVS): $90.8\% \pm 1.1\%$ indicates that the model is learning characteristics of the dataset that generalise and not only memorising artifacts.
The faithfulness measures (insertion: 0.72, deletion: 0.21) demonstrate that the visual explanations are genuinely loyal to the model's reasoning process rather than “after-the-fact” rationalisations. Grad-CAM++ produces more precise attention maps than Grad-CAM, which are notably beneficial for the detection of numerous lesions in illnesses. Attribution quality varied with symptom morphology, with distinct lesion-based diseases (Leaf Blast, Brown Spot) yielding sharper attributions than systemic diseases with diffuse symptoms. The pattern suggests that the quality of the explanation should take account of some of the qualities of an illness. The estimation of the uncertainty was well-calibrated (ECE: 0.087) and offered relevant decision support by means of confidence levels.
In terms of deployment, the computational demand is modest, and we report measured and estimated figures separately. \emph{Measured} (on the development workstation, NVIDIA RTX 3090, batch size 1): the classification model runs in 28\,ms at 28.6M parameters and $\approx$115\,MB storage; the attribution and uncertainty overheads are 0.5\,s for Grad-CAM++ and 0.8\,s for 30-pass Monte Carlo Dropout; and the 4-bit (NF4, GGUF) quantized Phi-3-mini, reduced from $\approx$7.6\,GB to 2.1\,GB, generates explanations in 1.2--2.0\,s on contemporary smartphones, enabling fully offline operation. \emph{Estimated} (not directly measured, device-dependent): in-field battery use is approximately 6--10\% per 10--20 diagnoses. On-device classifier latency on a phone or a Jetson Xavier NX edge module will differ from the workstation figure and should be benchmarked per target device. A practical trade-off remains: the offline LLM layer costs $\approx$2.1\,GB of storage and 1--2\,s of latency, which can exceed the budget of low-end devices; we therefore recommend a hybrid deployment in which the classifier, visual heatmap and uncertainty estimate (the safety-critical channels) always run on-device, while the natural-language layer is offline-optional or cloud-assisted. The modular architecture also allows easy integration with existing agricultural information systems, IoT sensors and precision-agriculture platforms.

\textbf{Limitations:} Some limitations need to be considered. Firstly, the assessment is based on the Paddy Doctor dataset of specific geographical regions and may not be generalizable to other rice growing areas with various climatic circumstances, cultivars or disease symptoms. Secondly, this work evaluates the system with automatic, model-based metrics only; a human-subjects study quantifying how the explanations affect agricultural stakeholders' diagnostic accuracy and trust has not yet been conducted, and is essential future work before any claim of end-user benefit can be made. Thirdly, in contrast to template based approaches, Phi-3-mini does not guarantee correctness in all cases of NLPG, and sometimes wrong words or statements are generated, hence prompt engineering and output checks must be employed carefully. Fourth, Monte Carlo Dropout can provide computationally cheap estimates of uncertainty, but it may miss some uncertainty types, such as uncertainty due to out-of-distribution inputs. Fifth, the 4-bit quantized LLM will require an additional 2.1GB of storage, which may be too much for some low-end devices. Finally, the focus on single-image diagnosis may not be adequate for scenarios where the farmers upload a sequence of photographs of the same plant or field to obtain a more thorough diagnosis.

\textbf{Future Directions:} There are several promising directions that should be pursued. There are methods for explaining more advanced, especially counterfactual and contrastive techniques which are specifically developed for agricultural contexts that could offer more intuitive explanations than current attention-based explanations. Combining multi-modal sensing (hyperspectral imaging and thermal cameras) may provide a huge improvement in diagnostics, particularly for diseases with subtle visual signs. Ongoing learning capabilities would enable the system to adjust to new variants of the disease and new environmental conditions without having to retrain the system completely. Federated learning models might help to improve the model collectively across different farms and regions, while maintaining data privacy, which could be essential for its implementation. Extension of other crop types is a logical next step, as is linking with precision agriculture technologies for automated early warning systems. Multilingual support will be crucial to disseminating to farmers across different linguistic areas and linkages to agricultural insurance schemes may offer extra motivation for uptake and offer useful data for risk assessment.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

In this work, we presented a comprehensive framework, ExplainableCropAI, that addresses the gap between performance and explainability. We integrated several explainable-AI techniques: Grad-CAM++ for visual explanations with improved localization, Monte Carlo Dropout for uncertainty quantification, and LLM-based natural language generation with Phi-3-mini for context-aware explanations. The system achieves 91.3\% classification accuracy with full explainability. Using the ConvNeXt V2-Tiny architecture with depthwise convolutions and GELU activations provided roughly 6\% higher accuracy than ResNet50-based approaches. Cross-validation stability ($90.8\% \pm 1.1\%$) indicates reliable performance that generalizes across diverse conditions. Visual explanations using Grad-CAM++ are faithful to the model's reasoning (insertion: 0.72, deletion: 0.21), uncertainty quantification produces well-calibrated confidence estimates (ECE: 0.087), and LLM-based natural language explanations stay close to the grounded knowledge-base references (BLEU: 0.73). Computational requirements remain practical for mobile deployment (28ms inference, 28.6M parameters for ConvNeXt V2-Tiny, approximately 115MB for the classification model plus 2.1GB for the quantized Phi-3-mini LLM).

Main key contributions of this work are as follows. First, it demonstrate empirically that comprehensive explainability can be integrated with agricultural AI without prohibitive performance penalties. Second, it contributes a reproducible benchmark protocol for evaluating visual-explanation faithfulness and uncertainty calibration in crop-disease imaging. Third, it presents a working technical framework that balances accuracy, explainability, and computational efficiency in deployable form. Fourth, our evaluation combines classification performance with automatic, model-based explanation-faithfulness and calibration metrics; a human-subjects evaluation of end-user benefit remains important future work.
